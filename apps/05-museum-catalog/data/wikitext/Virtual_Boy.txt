The '''Virtual Boy'''{{efn|{{Nihongo|バーチャルボーイ|Bācharu Bōi|lead=yes}}}} is a [[32-bit]] tabletop video game console developed and manufactured by [[Nintendo]]. Released in Japan on July 21, 1995, and in North America on August 14, 1995, it was promoted as the first console capable of rendering [[stereoscopic 3D]] graphics. The system features a red monochrome display viewed through a binocular neoprene visor mounted on a stand, with games employing a [[parallax]] effect to simulate depth. The console struggled commercially, and its poor market performance led Nintendo to discontinue production and software development in 1996 after releasing 22 games.

Development of the Virtual Boy spanned nearly four years at [[Nintendo Research & Development 1]] (R&D1) under general manager [[Gunpei Yokoi]], carrying the internal codename '''VR32''' and the product code '''VUE''' ("Virtual Utopia Experience"). Nintendo entered a licensing agreement with the American company Reflection Technology, Inc. (RTI) to utilize a stereoscopic [[LED]] eyepiece display that RTI had been developing since the 1980s, and constructed a dedicated manufacturing facility in China for mass production. Over the course of development, escalating production costs, health and legal concerns regarding head-mounted displays, and the diversion of company resources toward the [[Nintendo 64]] resulted in the project being scaled back into a stationary tabletop unit. Nintendo's lead game designer, [[Shigeru Miyamoto]], had minimal involvement with its software library, and the console was pushed to market in 1995 so Nintendo could concentrate on the Nintendo 64.

The Virtual Boy was panned by critics and became a [[List of commercial failures in video game consoles|commercial failure]] despite repeated price cuts. Its failure has been attributed to its high retail price, monochrome red-and-black visuals, limited stereoscopic immersion, awkward tabletop ergonomics, lack of true portability, and widespread reports of headaches, dizziness, and eye strain. With 770,000 units sold worldwide, the Virtual Boy is Nintendo's lowest-selling standalone console, far behind its second-lowest-selling system, the [[Wii U]], at 13.6 million units.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.nintendo.co.jp/ir/en/finance/hard_soft/index.html|title=IR Information : Sales Data – Hardware and Software Sales Units|website=Nintendo Co., Ltd.|access-date=March 11, 2021|archive-date=June 21, 2017|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170621033554/https://www.nintendo.co.jp/ir/en/finance/hard_soft/index.html|url-status=live}}</ref> Nintendo later revisited three-dimensional handheld hardware with the autostereoscopic [[Nintendo 3DS]] in 2011 and re-released the Virtual Boy library through the [[Nintendo Classics]] service for the [[Nintendo Switch]] and [[Nintendo Switch 2]] in 2026.

==History==
===Development===
Beginning in 1985, [[Massachusetts]]-based Reflection Technology, Inc. (RTI) developed a red LED eyepiece display technology called Scanned Linear Array.<ref name="GPro67"/><ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/> The company produced a stereoscopic [[Positional tracking|head-tracking]] display prototype called the ''Private Eye'', which featured a tank game.<ref name="EdwardsArs">{{Cite web |last=Edwards |first=Benj |date=May 15, 2024 |title=Virtual Boy: The bizarre rise and quick fall of Nintendo's enigmatic red console |url=https://arstechnica.com/gaming/2024/05/virtual-boy-the-bizarre-rise-and-quick-fall-of-nintendos-enigmatic-red-console/ |access-date=May 16, 2024 |website=[[Ars Technica]] |language=en-us}}</ref> Seeking funding and commercial partnerships, RTI demonstrated the ''Private Eye'' to several consumer electronics and toy manufacturers, including [[Mattel]] and [[Hasbro]].<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/> [[Sega]] passed on the technology due to its single-color display and concerns regarding [[motion sickness]].<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/><ref>{{cite web|url=http://revrob.com/sci-tech/264-tom-kalinske-talks-about-his-time-overseeing-sega-as-its-ceo-in-the-90s-reveals-that-sega-passed-on-virtual-boy-technology-considered-releasing-3do|title=Tom Kalinske Talks About His Time Overseeing Sega As Its CEO In the 90s; Reveals That Sega Passed On Virtual Boy Technology, Considered Releasing 3DO|last1=Vinciguerra|first1=Robert|website=The Rev. Rob Times|url-status=dead|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20151025155922/http://revrob.com/sci-tech/264-tom-kalinske-talks-about-his-time-overseeing-sega-as-its-ceo-in-the-90s-reveals-that-sega-passed-on-virtual-boy-technology-considered-releasing-3do|archive-date=October 25, 2015|access-date=September 21, 2015}}</ref>

Nintendo had previously explored virtual reality by funding British developer [[Argonaut Games]] with $1 million to design a full-color, motion-tracked headset codenamed the "Super Visor".<ref name="NL Super Visor">{{cite web |last=McFerran |first=Damien |date=November 27, 2019 |title=Feature: The Game-Changing Nintendo VR Headset That Never Was |url=https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2019/11/feature_the_game-changing_nintendo_vr_headset_that_never_was |website=[[Nintendo Life]] |access-date=October 18, 2026}}</ref> However, [[Gunpei Yokoi]], the general manager of [[Nintendo Research & Development 1|Nintendo R&D1]] and inventor of the [[Game & Watch]] and [[Game Boy]] handhelds, canceled Argonaut's project after enthusiastically receiving RTI's ''Private Eye'' demonstration.<ref name="NL Super Visor"/><ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/> Yokoi viewed RTI's display as a distinctive, cost-effective technology that competitors would find difficult to replicate, while enhancing Nintendo's reputation as a hardware innovator<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/><ref name="Boyer">{{cite magazine |last=Boyer |first=Steven |title=A Virtual Failure: Evaluating the Success of Nintendos Virtual Boy |magazine=[[Velvet Light Trap]] |issue=64 |date=Fall 2009 |volume=64 |pages=23–33 |doi=10.1353/vlt.0.0039 |s2cid=190682178 |url=https://archive.org/details/VirtualFailure/mode/2up}}</ref> and encouraging greater creativity in game design.<ref name="Ultimate History of Video Games"/>{{rp|514}} Codenaming the project "VR32" (for "Virtual Reality 32-bit")<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/> and assigning it the internal product code "VUE" (standing for "Virtual Utopia Experience"),<ref name="NL Codenames">{{cite web |last=Lane |first=Gavin |date=December 25, 2020 |title=Feature: Nintendo Console Codenames And Product Codes |url=https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2020/12/feature_nintendo_console_codenames_and_product_codes |website=[[Nintendo Life]] |access-date=October 18, 2026}}</ref> Nintendo entered into an exclusive agreement with RTI to license the display.<ref name="GPro67">{{cite magazine|url=https://retrocdn.net/images/c/c7/GamePro_US_067.pdf|title=April Brings Virtual Boy|date=February 1995|magazine=[[GamePro]]|url-status=live|issue=67|page=162|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181007040143/https://retrocdn.net/images/c/c7/GamePro_US_067.pdf|archive-date=October 7, 2018}}</ref> While [[Nintendo Research & Development 3]] (R&D3) focused on developing the [[Nintendo 64]], Nintendo's other engineering divisions were free to experiment with auxiliary hardware.<ref name="Boyer" />

Spending nearly four years in development and constructing a dedicated manufacturing plant in China,<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/> Nintendo worked to turn the VR32 concept into an affordable consumer product. Yokoi retained RTI's red LEDs because they were the least expensive option, consumed minimal battery power,<ref name="Boyer" /> and provided complete blackness behind the lit pixels to heighten the sensation of infinite depth, unlike a backlit LCD.<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/> RTI and Nintendo stated that a full-color display would have caused "jumpy images in tests" and proved prohibitively expensive,<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/><ref name="Guardian Super Mario"/> pushing the retail price past {{USD|500|1995|long=yes|round=-1}}.<ref name="Ultimate History of Video Games"/>{{rp|514}} Facing internal concerns over motion sickness, the risk of young children developing [[amblyopia]] (lazy eye), the prospect of facial injuries if a wearer collided with objects, and Japan's Product Liability Act of 1995, Nintendo eliminated head-tracking functionality and converted the head-mounted goggle design into a stationary, steel-shielded tabletop unit mounted on a stand, following recommendations from the Schepens Eye Research Institute.<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/><ref name="Ultimate History of Video Games"/>{{rp|514}}

{{blockquote|[W]e experimented with a color LCD screen, but the users did not see depth, they just saw double. Color graphics give people the impression that a game is high tech. But just because a game has a beautiful display does not mean that the game is fun to play. ... Red uses less battery and red is easier to recognize. That is why red is used for traffic lights.|Gunpei Yokoi<ref name="Ultimate History of Video Games"/>{{rp|514}}}}

Because internal resources were increasingly prioritized for the flagship Nintendo 64, and lead designer [[Shigeru Miyamoto]] had minimal involvement, Virtual Boy software was developed without Nintendo's full attention.<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/> According to [[David Sheff]]'s book ''[[Game Over (Sheff book)|Game Over]]'', an increasingly reluctant Yokoi did not intend for the downscaled Virtual Boy to be released in its final form, but Nintendo pushed the console to market so development staff could shift focus to the Nintendo 64.<ref name="Game Over by David Sheff">{{cite book | last1=Sheff|first1=David|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=b_N5FzzD3hsC|title=Game Over: How Nintendo Zapped an American Industry, Captured Your Dollars, and Enslaved Your Children|last2=Eddy|first2=Andy|publisher=GamePress|year=1999|isbn=978-0-9669617-0-6|oclc=26214063|author-link=David Sheff}}</ref>

===Release===
''[[The New York Times]]'' previewed the Virtual Boy on November 13, 1994,<ref name="NYT Nintendo counts">{{cite news|first=John | last=Markoff|url=https://www.nytimes.com/1994/11/14/business/nintendo-counts-on-a-new-virtual-game.html|title=Nintendo Counts on a New 'Virtual' Game|newspaper=The New York Times|page=2|date=November 14, 1994|access-date=August 25, 2020 | url-status=live | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180205015527/http://www.nytimes.com/1994/11/14/business/nintendo-counts-on-a-new-virtual-game.html | archive-date=February 5, 2018}}</ref> and Nintendo officially announced the console via press release the following day, promising that the system would "totally immerse players into their own private universe".<ref name="press1">{{cite web |url=http://www.planetvb.com/modules/advertising/?r17 |title=Nintendo introduces video game players to three-dimensional worlds with new virtual reality video game system « Press Releases « Planet Virtual Boy |publisher=Planetvb.com |access-date=November 18, 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180628124825/https://www.planetvb.com/modules/advertising/?r17 |archive-date=June 28, 2018 |url-status=live }}</ref> Initial press releases and interviews emphasized the hardware's technological capabilities while avoiding specific details about upcoming games.<ref name="Boyer" /> Nintendo demonstrated the console on November 15 and 16 at its [[Nintendo Space World#Shoshinkai 1994|Shoshinkai 1994]] trade show in Japan,<ref name="NP Jan 1995 VB"/><ref name="Boyer" /> followed by a North American demonstration by [[Nintendo of America]] at the Winter Consumer Electronics Show on January 6, 1995.<ref name="press1" />

Even with cost-saving measures in place, Nintendo priced the Virtual Boy at a relatively high {{USD|179.95|1994|round=-1}}.<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma">{{cite web|url=http://www.fastcompany.com/3050016/unraveling-the-enigma-of-nintendos-virtual-boy-20-years-later|title=Unraveling The Enigma Of Nintendo's Virtual Boy, 20 Years Later|last=Edwards|first=Benj|date=August 21, 2015|work=[[Fast Company]]|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180707231258/https://www.fastcompany.com/3050016/unraveling-the-enigma-of-nintendos-virtual-boy-20-years-later|archive-date=July 7, 2018|access-date=December 21, 2015}}</ref><ref name="Boyer" /><ref name="Ultimate History of Video Games">{{cite book |last=Kent |first=Steven L. |author-link=Steven L. Kent |title=The Ultimate History of Video Games: The Story Behind the Craze that Touched our Lives and Changed the World |date=2002 |publisher=Random House International |location=New York |isbn=978-0-7615-3643-7 |oclc=59416169 |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=PTrcTeAqeaEC |pages=513–515, 518, 519, 523, 524 |access-date=October 20, 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170709060608/https://books.google.com/books?id=PTrcTeAqeaEC&printsec=frontcover |archive-date=July 9, 2017 |url-status=live }}</ref>{{rp|513}} Although slightly less expensive and less powerful than a home console, it was substantially more costly than the handheld Game Boy. Despite its more advanced 32-bit graphics, the Virtual Boy was not designed to replace the Game Boy in Nintendo's product line, because operating the unit requires a flat surface and completely obstructs the player's peripheral vision. ''[[Design News]]'' described the Virtual Boy as a technological evolution of the [[View-Master]] 3D image viewer.<ref>{{cite magazine |title=BreakTime: Virtual Boy Updates the Viewmaster Idea |magazine=[[Design News]] |volume=50 |issue=6 |year=1995 |page=192}}</ref>

The Virtual Boy launched on July 21, 1995, in Japan and on August 14, 1995, in North America,<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/><ref name="launchdate">{{cite press release |title=VIRTUAL BOY LAUNCH DATE ANNOUNCED |url=https://www.virtual-boy.com/events/e3-1995/articles/733039/ |publisher=Nintendo of America |date=May 11, 1995 |access-date=February 15, 2023}}</ref> accompanied in the latter region by the [[launch game]]s ''[[Mario's Tennis]]'', ''[[Red Alarm]]'', ''[[Teleroboxer]]'', and ''[[Galactic Pinball]]''.<ref name="LAT">{{cite news|last=Curtiss|first=Aaron|url=https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1995-08-31-ca-40702-story.html?_amp=true|title=Valley Weekend; VIDEO GAMES; Virtual Boy a Blend of Familiar and Strange; although Hardware for the Latest Nintendo Offering is Odd and Cumbersome, the Play Action is Big and Loud|work=[[Los Angeles Times]]|page=14|date=August 31, 1995|access-date=May 24, 2012|archive-date=March 8, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210308122012/https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1995-08-31-ca-40702-story.html?_amp=true|url-status=live}}</ref> It followed in Latin American markets in September 1995, including a Brazilian release on September 18,<ref name=estado /><ref>{{cite journal |title=Virtual Boy |journal=Club Nintendo Colombia |page=2 |issue=9/1995 }}</ref> and was never released in PAL territories. In North America, Nintendo bundled ''[[Mario's Tennis]]'' with every Virtual Boy unit as a [[pack-in game]].<ref>{{cite web |url=https://www.flickr.com/photos/dionisio/2833237521/sizes/o/in/photostream/ |title=Virtual Boy 'Third Dimension' Ad (1995)|publisher=Flickr |access-date=November 18, 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150904094440/https://www.flickr.com/photos/dionisio/2833237521/sizes/o/in/photostream/ |archive-date=September 4, 2015 |url-status=live }}</ref> The console arrived on the market later than competing 32-bit systems such as the [[PlayStation (console)|PlayStation]], [[3DO Interactive Multiplayer|3DO]], and [[Sega Saturn]], though at a lower introductory price.<ref name="Guardian Super Mario" />

Marketing targeted an older demographic, departing from Nintendo's traditional child-focused approach, and promoted the system as a form of virtual reality while emphasizing hardware specifications over specific software titles.<ref name="Boyer" />

Books]]|access-date=August 25, 2020|archive-date=February 7, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230207095407/https://books.google.com/books?id=wAsEAAAAMBAJ&pg=PA98|url-status=live}}</ref> Rather than building word-of-mouth momentum, the rental program harmed the console's long-term prospects by allowing players to experience its limited immersion firsthand before buying.<ref name="Boyer" /> By mid-1996, Blockbuster was liquidating its rental inventory at $50 per unit,<ref name="EGM86">{{cite magazine|date=September 1996|title=Gaming Gossip|url=https://retrocdn.net/images/2/2e/EGM_US_086.pdf|magazine=[[Electronic Gaming Monthly]]|issue=86|page=34|access-date=September 12, 2020|archive-date=August 21, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190821193530/https://retrocdn.net/images/2/2e/EGM_US_086.pdf|url-status=live}}</ref> and the broader marketing campaign was widely regarded as a failure.<ref name="Tomb Space">{{cite book |last1=King |first1=Geoff |first2=Tanya |last2=Krzywinska |year=2006 |title=Tomb Raiders and Space Invaders: Videogame Forms and Contexts |publisher=[[I.B. Tauris]] |isbn=978-1-84511-108-3}}</ref>

==Hardware==
The Virtual Boy's [[central processing unit|CPU]] is an [[NEC]] [[V850#V810 (obsoleted)|V810]] 32-bit [[RISC]] processor clocked at 20 MHz,<ref name="GPro67"/> making the console Nintendo's first 32-bit system.<ref name="Guardian Super Mario" /> The unit contains a pair of 1×224 linear LED arrays (one per eye) and rapidly scans each array across the user's field of view using flat oscillating mirrors. Because these mirrors vibrate back and forth at high speed, the console emits an audible mechanical hum during operation. Every official Virtual Boy game cartridge includes an optional automatic-pause setting that prompts the player to take a break every 15 to 30 minutes to reduce eye strain. Built-in speakers on either side of the unit provide stereo audio.<ref name="Powell">{{cite news|last=Powell | first=Doug|title=A Virtual Backlash|work=Computing Canada|date=December 21, 1994|pages=1, 4|publisher=ABI/INFORM Global}}</ref>

===Display===
[[File:Virtual-Boy-Eyes.jpg|thumb|The screens of the Virtual Boy]]
The Virtual Boy is the first video game console capable of displaying stereoscopic 3D graphics, which Nintendo marketed as a form of virtual reality.<ref>{{cite web |url=https://gizmodo.com/tag/virtual-boy |title=Virtual boy News, Videos, Reviews and Gossip |website=Gizmodo |access-date=June 20, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150428003847/http://gizmodo.com/tag/virtual-boy |archive-date=April 28, 2015 |url-status=live }}</ref> Whereas conventional video games rely on [[monocular cues]] to suggest three dimensions on a flat screen, the Virtual Boy generates an illusion of depth through [[parallax]]. Similar to a [[head-mounted display]], the user peers into a [[neoprene]] eyeshade on the front of the visor, where twin eyeglass-style lenses project the [[Grayscale|monochromatic]] red image, adjusted via focus and interpupillary distance dials on top of the housing.<ref name="EngadgetReview">{{Cite web | url=https://www.engadget.com/2011-03-21-nintendo-virtual-boy-review.html | title=Nintendo Virtual Boy review | first=Tim | last=Stevens | date=March 21, 2011 | website=Engadget | access-date=August 30, 2020 | archive-date=April 8, 2020 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200408081117/https://www.engadget.com/2011-03-21-nintendo-virtual-boy-review.html | url-status=live }}</ref>

The display produces two 2-bit (four-shade) monochrome red images at a resolution of 384×224 pixels<ref name="EngadgetReview"/> and a [[frame rate]] of approximately 50.27 [[Hertz|Hz]].<ref>{{Cite web |url=http://tasvideos.org/PlatformFramerates.html |title=Platform Framerates |website=tasvideos.org |access-date=March 4, 2020 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200229184750/http://tasvideos.org/PlatformFramerates.html |archive-date=February 29, 2020 |url-status=live}}</ref> An oscillating mirror transforms each single vertical column of 224 red LEDs into a full horizontal field of pixels. Nintendo stated that a color display would have produced "jumpy" visuals and been prohibitively costly,<ref name="Guardian Super Mario" /> as full color required red, green, and blue LEDs at a time when blue LEDs were still expensive.

===Controller===
[[File:Virtual-Boy-Controller.jpg|thumb|The Virtual Boy controller with the slide-on battery pack attached]]
The Virtual Boy requires the player to sit at a table,<ref name="NYT Nintendo counts" /><ref>{{cite magazine|date=March 1995|title=Virtual Places in Small Spaces|page=24|magazine=[[GamePro]]|issue=68|url=https://retrocdn.net/images/0/07/GamePro_US_068.pdf|access-date=September 12, 2020|archive-date=January 13, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200113193151/https://retrocdn.net/images/0/07/GamePro_US_068.pdf|url-status=live}}</ref> as a promised harness for standing play was never released.<ref name="Guardian Super Mario">{{cite news|first=Kevin|last=Rafferty|title=Super Mario Takes Leap into Three Dimensional Space|newspaper=The Guardian|page=0|date=November 16, 1994|url=https://www.proquest.com/docview/294877556|access-date=August 25, 2020|archive-date=February 7, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230207095416/https://www.proquest.com/docview/294877556/4C825E3013B347F4PQ|id={{ProQuest|294877556}} |url-status=live}}</ref> Because the system emphasizes three-dimensional movement along a Z-axis, its M-shaped controller—reminiscent of the later [[Nintendo 64 controller]]—implements dual digital [[D-pad]]s (one on each side) alongside four front face buttons arranged symmetrically, with Select and Start on the left and the B and A buttons on the right, plus two rear shoulder buttons (L and R) positioned behind the directional pads.<ref name="EngadgetReview"/><ref>{{cite web |url=http://mynintendonews.com/2013/11/30/digital-foundry-vita-remote-play-isnt-quite-as-good-as-the-wii-u-gamepad/ |title=Digital Foundry: Vita Remote Play Isn't Quite As Good As The Wii U GamePad |publisher=My Nintendo News |date=November 30, 2013 |access-date=June 20, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140714204236/http://mynintendonews.com/2013/11/30/digital-foundry-vita-remote-play-isnt-quite-as-good-as-the-wii-u-gamepad/ |archive-date=July 14, 2014 |url-status=live }}</ref> The controller also houses the console's main power switch on its front face and serves as the system's power supply routing point via a slide-on rear attachment that holds six [[AA battery|AA batteries]] or an AC adapter tap.<ref name="EngadgetReview"/>

In traditional two-dimensional games, the two directional pads function interchangeably.<ref name="NL Making">{{cite web |last=McFerran |first=Damien |url=http://www.nintendolife.com/news/2010/03/feature_the_making_of_the_nintendo_virtual_boy |title=Feature: The Making of the Nintendo Virtual Boy |date=March 26, 2010 |website=[[Nintendo Life]] |access-date=June 20, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140116051432/http://www.nintendolife.com/news/2010/03/feature_the_making_of_the_nintendo_virtual_boy |archive-date=January 16, 2014 |url-status=live }}</ref> In games built around three-dimensional movement or dual-input mechanics, such as ''[[Red Alarm]]'', ''[[3D Tetris]]'', ''[[Teleroboxer]]'', and ''[[Mario Clash]]'', each pad controls a separate function.<ref name="NL Making"/><ref name="Kolan"/> The symmetrical layout also enables left-handed players to reverse the controls, similar to the [[Atari Lynx]].<ref>{{cite book |url=https://books.google.com/books?id=HaP1LUYyvT4C&q=%22atari+lynx%22+interchangeable+pads&pg=PT137 |title=The Official GameSalad Guide to Game Development |via=Google Books |date=April 12, 2013 |access-date=June 20, 2014 |isbn=978-1133605645 |publisher=Gamesalad |last1=Novak |first1=Jeannie |archive-date=February 7, 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230207095409/https://books.google.com/books?id=HaP1LUYyvT4C&q=%22atari+lynx%22+interchangeable+pads&pg=PT137 |url-status=live }}</ref>

===Connectivity===
During development, Nintendo promised system-to-system connectivity for competitive multiplayer play.<ref name="Guardian Super Mario"/> A Virtual Boy link cable remained under development at Nintendo as late as the third quarter of 1996.<ref name="EGM86"/> However, the console's 8-pin EXT (extension) port, located on the underside of the visor housing below the controller port, was never officially supported at retail. Multiplayer features utilizing the EXT port were stripped from ''[[Waterworld (Virtual Boy video game)|Waterworld]]'' prior to release,<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.vr32.de/modules/interviews/?3 |title=Steve Woita « Interviews « Planet Virtual Boy |publisher=Vr32.de |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20071109190056/http://www.vr32.de/modules/interviews/?3|archive-date=November 9, 2007 |date=November 23, 1993 |access-date=October 17, 2013}}</ref> and a planned conversion of ''[[Faceball 2000|Faceball]]'' was canceled.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.planetvb.com/modules/games/?u005g |title=Faceball « Games « Planet Virtual Boy |publisher=Planetvb.com |access-date=June 20, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140703233012/http://www.planetvb.com/modules/games/?u005g |archive-date=July 3, 2014 |url-status=live }}</ref>

==Games==
{{Main|List of Virtual Boy games}}
[[File:Mario's Tennis screenshot.png|thumb|''[[Mario's Tennis]]'', the North American pack-in for Virtual Boy, is converted by an [[Video game console emulator|emulator]] to anaglyphic red and blue format, to simulate the Virtual Boy's stereoscopic display on a 2D display.<br />{{3d glasses}}]]
Nintendo initially showcased three launch games and planned to release two or three titles per month thereafter.<ref name="Guardian Super Mario"/> Given the system's brief lifespan, only 22 commercial games were released in total, comprising 19 in Japan and 14 in North America.<ref name="Kolan">{{cite web |last=Kolan |first=Patrick |title=IGN Retro: Virtual Boy's Best Games |website=[[IGN]] |date=January 14, 2008 |access-date=January 21, 2009 |url=http://retro.ign.com/articles/845/845487p1.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090123101614/http://retro.ign.com/articles/845/845487p1.html |archive-date=January 23, 2009 |url-status=live }}</ref> Third-party support was heavily restricted compared to earlier Nintendo platforms. According to Gunpei Yokoi, Nintendo president [[Hiroshi Yamauchi]] dictated that only a small group of selected third-party developers be shown the hardware prior to its formal unveiling in order to prevent low-quality software from saturating the system.<ref>{{cite magazine |date=April 1995|title=An Audience with... Gumpei Yokoi |magazine=[[Next Generation (magazine)|Next Generation]]|issue=4|pages=44–46 |url=https://archive.org/details/nextgen-issue-004/page/n45/mode/2up}}</ref>

When asked in 2010 whether Virtual Boy games would be made available for download via the [[Virtual Console]] service on the [[Nintendo 3DS]], Nintendo of America president [[Reggie Fils-Aimé]] stated that he could not answer because he had minimal familiarity with the platform, noting that it would be difficult for him to make a case for their inclusion.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://kotaku.com/#!5690257/argue-for-a-virtual-boy-store-on-the-nintendo-3ds-if-you-can|title=Kotaku – The Gamer's Guide|publisher=Gawker Media|work=Kotaku|access-date=December 21, 2015|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101106085901/http://kotaku.com/#!5690257/argue-for-a-virtual-boy-store-on-the-nintendo-3ds-if-you-can|archive-date=November 6, 2010|url-status=live}}</ref>

The hobbyist community at ''Planet Virtual Boy'' subsequently developed homebrew Virtual Boy software.<ref name="Boyer" /> Three previously unreleased games—''[[Bound High!|Bound High]]'', ''Niko-Chan Battle'' (the Japanese localization of ''[[Faceball 2000|Faceball]]''), and ''Virtual League Baseball 2'' (a sequel to ''[[Virtual League Baseball]]'')—were later leaked online. Two additional unreleased first-party games, ''[[Dragon Hopper]]'' and ''[[Zero Racers]]'', were officially released through the [[Nintendo Classics]] service in 2026.<ref name=Uncancelled/>

==Reception==
[[File:Virtual Boy - Akihabara, Otaku Style.jpg|thumb|Using the Virtual Boy eyepiece]]
The Virtual Boy garnered largely negative reviews from critics and was a commercial failure.<ref>{{cite web |author=Lisa Foiles |date=March 1, 2011 |title=Top 5 Hardware Super Fails |work=The Escapist |url=http://www.escapistmagazine.com/videos/view/top-5-with-lisa-foiles/2796-Top-5-Hardware-Super-Fails |access-date=June 20, 2014 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180707201630/http://www.escapistmagazine.com/videos/view/top-5-with-lisa-foiles/2796-Top-5-Hardware-Super-Fails |archive-date=July 7, 2018 |url-status=live }}</ref> Commentators attributed its failure to its high price, physical discomfort during play, and an ineffective marketing campaign.<ref name="Tomb Space" />

Attendees who previewed the hardware at Shoshinkai 1994 complained that the ''Mario'' demonstration lacked realism, omitted full color, and failed to [[Match moving|motion-track]] the image when players turned their heads.<ref name="Guardian Super Mario" /> In the lead editorial of ''[[Electronic Gaming Monthly]]'' following the event, Ed Semrad predicted that the Virtual Boy would suffer poor launch sales due to its monochrome display, lack of true portability, uninspiring software lineup, and high price relative to the experience offered.<ref>{{cite magazine|last=Semrad|first=Ed|date=January 1995|title=Nintendo Stumbles with Virtual Boy Intro!|url=https://retrocdn.net/images/8/8d/EGM_US_066.pdf|magazine=[[Electronic Gaming Monthly]]|issue=66|page=6|access-date=September 13, 2020|archive-date=October 7, 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181007040332/https://retrocdn.net/images/8/8d/EGM_US_066.pdf|url-status=live}}</ref> Editors at ''[[Next Generation (magazine)|Next Generation]]'' similarly questioned the console's prospects after the show, concluding their feature by asking, "But who will buy it? It's not portable, it's awkward to use, it's 100% antisocial (unlike multiplayer [[SNES]]/[[Sega Genesis|Genesis]] games), it's too expensive and – most importantly – the 'VR' (i.e. 3D effect) doesn't add to the game at all: it's just a novelty."<ref>{{cite magazine|date=March 1995|title=Nintendo Pins Hopes on Virtual Boy|magazine=[[Next Generation (magazine)|Next Generation]]|issue=3|pages=20–21 |url=https://archive.org/details/nextgen-issue-003/page/n21/mode/2up}}</ref>

Post-release reviews praised the console's novelty while questioning its practical purpose and long-term viability. ''[[Los Angeles Times|The Los Angeles Times]]'' described the gameplay as "at once familiar and strange", praising the sense of motion and graphics while criticizing the hardware as tedious and non-portable.<ref name="LAT" /> In a May 1996 follow-up column, the same reviewer found the console [[asociality|asocial]] to play but expressed hope for its growing library.<ref name="LAT2">{{cite news|last=Curtiss|first=Aaron|title=VALLEY WEEKEND; Nintendo Virtual Boy Measures Up to Billing; as its Library of Titles Slowly Grows, the 3-D System is Becoming More Well-Rounded and Less of a Headache|page=15|work=Los Angeles Times|date=May 2, 1996|url=https://www.proquest.com/docview/293327145|access-date=August 26, 2020|archive-date=February 7, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230207095409/https://www.proquest.com/docview/293327145/F8C1FD056543408EPQ|id={{ProQuest|293327145}} |url-status=live}}</ref> Reviewing the console shortly after its North American launch, ''Next Generation'' wrote, "Unusual and innovative, the Virtual Boy can be seen as a gamble in the same way that the [[Game Boy]] was, but it's a lot harder to see the VB succeeding to the same world-conquering extent that the Game Boy did." The magazine praised the sharp display and 3D effect, but noted that the monochrome visuals and concerns over vision damage in young players restricted its appeal. It also observed that while the software library was serviceable, it failed to leverage Nintendo's flagship franchises because ''[[The Legend of Zelda]]'' and ''[[Metroid]]'' were absent, the ''Mario'' entries departed from the series's core platforming formula, and the system lacked a killer app comparable to the Game Boy's ''[[Tetris (Game Boy)|Tetris]]''.<ref name="NGen12">{{cite magazine|title=Which Game System is the Best!?|magazine=[[Next Generation (magazine)|Next Generation]]|issue=12|date=December 1995|pages=36–85|url=https://www.retromags.com/magazines/usa/next-generation/next-generation-issue-12/|access-date=August 26, 2020|archive-date=August 9, 2020|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200809173346/https://www.retromags.com/magazines/usa/next-generation/next-generation-issue-12/|url-status=live}}</ref>

Although Nintendo had promised a virtual reality experience, critics noted that the monochrome display limited immersion<ref name="Boyer" /><ref name="NGen12"/> and frequently dismissed the three-dimensional depth as a gimmick layered onto fundamentally two-dimensional<ref name="Boyer" /> or one-dimensional gameplay.<ref name="WaPo">{{cite news|last=Frischling | first=Bill|title=Sideline Play|newspaper=The Washington Post|page=11|date=October 25, 1995}}</ref> ''[[The Washington Post]]'' remarked that even when a game conveyed three-dimensionality, it suffered from "hollow vector graphics".<ref name="WaPo" /> Yokoi felt the hardware was best suited to action and puzzle games,<ref name="NGen12"/> even though those genres offered minimal spatial immersion. Multiple critics<ref name="Boyer" /><ref name="Tomb Space" /> lamented the absence of head-tracking, noting that players interacted with the game world solely through a traditional hand controller rather than physical movement. Steven Boyer observed that the console "struggles to merge the two distinct media forms of home consoles and virtual reality devices", employing basic stereoscopic optics without bodily feedback.<ref name="Boyer" />

Many reviewers reported physiological discomfort during play. Bill Frischling of ''The Washington Post'' experienced "dizziness, nausea and headaches",<ref name="WaPo" /> which critics attributed to both the stark red LEDs and the hunched posture required to use the tabletop stand. Contemporary articles in ''[[Electronic Engineering Times]]'' and [[CMP Media]]'s ''TechWeb'' speculated that prolonged use of immersive headsets like the Virtual Boy could trigger motion sickness, [[Flashback (psychology)|flashbacks]], and [[permanent brain damage]].<ref>{{cite magazine|date=September 1995|title=VR Headsets Get Warning|url=https://archive.org/details/GamePro_Issue_074_September_1995/page/n141/mode/2up|magazine=[[GamePro]]|publisher=[[International Data Group|IDG]]|issue=84|page=140}}</ref> In subsequent years, Nintendo executives spoke frankly about its commercial reception, with Nintendo of America chairman [[Howard Lincoln]] stating flatly that the Virtual Boy "just failed".<ref name="Boyer" /> In a 2025 retrospective, journalist John Szczepaniak reported experiencing temporary [[color blindness]] after playing ''[[Waterworld (Virtual Boy video game)|Waterworld]]'' for two hours without taking the recommended 15-minute breaks.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Szczepaniak|first=John|date=September 17, 2025|title="I Still Think The Virtual Boy Was Probably Just Too Ahead Of Its Time" - Japanese Developers On Nintendo's Most Infamous Flop|url=https://www.timeextension.com/features/i-still-think-the-virtual-boy-was-probably-just-too-ahead-of-its-time-japanese-developers-on-nintendos-most-infamous-flop|url-status=live|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20250917231549/https://www.timeextension.com/features/i-still-think-the-virtual-boy-was-probably-just-too-ahead-of-its-time-japanese-developers-on-nintendos-most-infamous-flop|archive-date=September 17, 2025|access-date=June 29, 2026|website=[[Time Extension]]|language=en-GB}}</ref>

==Legacy==
According to ''Game Over'', Nintendo attributed the console's failure directly to its creator, Gunpei Yokoi.<ref name="Game Over by David Sheff"/> The commercial failure of the Virtual Boy was widely reported as a factor in Yokoi's resignation from Nintendo in August 1996, though he had planned to retire years earlier and remained at the company to complete the successful [[Game Boy Pocket]] prior to his departure.<ref name="N-Sider Profiles">{{cite web|url=http://www.n-sider.com/contentview.php?contentid=222|title=N-Sider Profiles|access-date=August 19, 2008|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20040404130954/http://n-sider.com/articleview.php?articleid=270|archive-date=April 4, 2004|url-status=live}}</ref> According to his colleague Yoshihiro Taki at Nintendo and Koto, Yokoi had originally intended to retire at age 50 to pursue independent projects and had merely postponed the decision.<ref name="Nintendo Magic">{{cite book | first=Osamu | last=Inoue | others=Paul Tuttle Starr (translator) | date=April 27, 2010 | title=Nintendo Magic: Winning the Videogame Wars | publisher=Vertical | isbn=978-1934287224 }}</ref> Nintendo maintained that Yokoi's departure was "absolutely coincidental" to the commercial performance of any hardware,<ref>{{cite magazine|date=November 1996|title=Nintendo's Leap into the Unknown|url=https://retrocdn.net/images/e/ed/NextGeneration_US_23.pdf|magazine=[[Next Generation (magazine)|Next Generation]]|publisher=[[Imagine Media]]|issue=23|page=16|access-date=September 13, 2020|archive-date=June 22, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210622012540/https://retrocdn.net/images/e/ed/NextGeneration_US_23.pdf|url-status=live}}</ref> and ''The New York Times'' reported that Yokoi retained a close relationship with the company.<ref>{{cite news | url=https://query.nytimes.com/gst/fullpage.html?res=9D05E4D7173CF93AA35753C1A961958260 | title=Gunpei Yokoi, Chief Designer Of Game Boy, Is Dead at 56 | access-date=August 19, 2008 | work=The New York Times | first=Andrew | last=Pollack | date=October 9, 1997 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090130092138/http://query.nytimes.com/gst/fullpage.html?res=9D05E4D7173CF93AA35753C1A961958260 | archive-date=January 30, 2009 | url-status=live }}</ref> After leaving Nintendo, Yokoi founded Koto Laboratory and collaborated with [[Bandai]] to design the [[WonderSwan]], a handheld system competing with the Game Boy.

The Virtual Boy's emphasis on spatial peripherals and hardware experimentation resurfaced in later console generations,<ref>{{cite magazine |author=Anonymous |title=Introduction |magazine=[[Velvet Light Trap]] |issue=64 |date=Fall 2009 |pages=1–2 |url=https://www.proquest.com/docview/222843422 |id={{ProQuest|222843422}} |access-date=April 24, 2021 |archive-date=February 7, 2023 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230207095407/https://www.proquest.com/docview/222843422 |url-status=live }}</ref> while display licensor Reflection Technology, Inc. was financially devastated by the system's cancellation and wound down operations by 1997.<ref name="Unraveling the Enigma"/> Ahead of the 2011 launch of the [[Nintendo 3DS]], which features [[Autostereoscopy|autostereoscopic]] 3D visuals without glasses, Shigeru Miyamoto reflected on the Virtual Boy's limitations. He observed that although the hardware could render wireframe graphics, its games primarily used depth-separated two-dimensional planes, and noted that he had ruled out wireframe player characters during Nintendo 64 development as too visually sparse. Miyamoto added that he viewed the Virtual Boy as a novelty that should not have prominently carried the main Nintendo brand.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/#/3ds/how-nintendo-3ds-made/0/1 |title=Shigeru Miyamoto Talks About Virtual Boy |publisher=Iwataasks.nintendo.com |access-date=July 30, 2011 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190622234955/http://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/#/3ds/how-nintendo-3ds-made/0/1 |archive-date=June 22, 2019 |url-status=live }}</ref>

In February 2016, Nintendo president [[Tatsumi Kimishima]] stated that the company was researching virtual reality but required additional time to assess the technology.<ref>{{cite tweet|user=mochi_wsj|author=Takashi Mochizuki|number=694530845365768194|date=February 2, 2016|title=(cont'd) I guess Nintendo isn't planning anything VR in the near future because he said more time and efforts needed to assess technology.|access-date=October 10, 2017}}</ref> In a February 2017 interview with ''[[The Nikkei]]'', Kimishima added that Nintendo would bring VR to the [[Nintendo Switch]] once it determined how users could play comfortably for extended sessions.<ref>{{cite tweet|user=serkantoto|author=Dr. Serkan Toto|number=826841955111890944|date=February 1, 2017|title=Just in: Nintendo president Kimishima tells the Nikkei Switch's online service will be priced at 2-3,000 yen ($17.50 – $26.50) *YEARLY*.|access-date=October 10, 2017}}</ref> In 2019, Nintendo released a cardboard VR Goggles kit for the Switch as part of its [[Nintendo Labo]] line, where the console's tablet screen is slotted into a handheld viewer fitted with stereoscopic lenses.<ref>{{cite web |last=Marshall |first=Cass |date=March 6, 2019 |title=Nintendo brings VR to Switch with new Labo kit |url=https://www.polygon.com/2019/3/6/18254014/nintendo-switch-vr-labo-kit-release-date |access-date=March 6, 2019 |work=[[Polygon (website)|Polygon]]}}</ref>
