In the [[history of video games]], the '''first generation''' era includes video games, [[video game console]]s, and [[handheld video game console]]s available from 1972 to 1983. Notable consoles of the first generation include the [[Odyssey series]] (excluding the [[Magnavox Odyssey 2]]), the [[Pong#Home version|Atari Home Pong]],<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.gamasutra.com/view/feature/130414/the_history_of_atari_19711977.php?print=1|title=The History of Atari: 1971–1977|last=Fulton|first=Steve|date=November 6, 2007|website=Gamasutra|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180912021902/http://www.gamasutra.com/view/feature/130414/the_history_of_atari_19711977.php?print=1|archive-date=September 12, 2018|url-status=dead|access-date=February 26, 2019}}</ref> the [[Coleco Telstar series]] and the [[Color TV-Game series]]. The generation ended with the [[Computer TV-Game]] in 1980 and its following discontinuation in 1983, but many manufacturers had left the market prior due to the market decline in the year of 1978 and the start of the [[second generation of video game consoles]].

Most of the games developed during this generation were hard-wired into the consoles and unlike later generations, most were not contained on removable media that the user could switch between.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=_VlmDwAAQBAJ&q=odyssey+basic+graphics&pg=PA17|title=Video Games|last=Hile|first=Kevin|date=October 26, 2009|publisher=Greenhaven Publishing LLC|isbn=9781420503067|language=en|access-date=February 26, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190227060542/https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=_VlmDwAAQBAJ&pg=PA17&dq=odyssey+basic+graphics&hl=en&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwjgpOm7udrgAhXvSRUIHTdDAkcQ6AEINzAC#v=onepage&q=odyssey%20basic%20graphics&f=false|archive-date=February 27, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> Consoles often came with accessories and cartridges that could alter the way the game played to enhance the gameplay experience<ref name="BtC-EVGH">{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=oK3D4i5ldKgC&q=pong|title=Before the Crash: Early Video Game History|last=Wolf|first=Mark J. P.|date=June 15, 2012|publisher=Wayne State University Press|isbn=9780814337226 |access-date=February 26, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190227061837/https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=oK3D4i5ldKgC&printsec=frontcover&dq=video+game+console+odyssey&hl=en&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwiJmL3Yt9rgAhVHRxUIHTn4Aa8Q6AEIQDAE#v=onepage&q=pong&f=false|archive-date=February 27, 2019|url-status=live |pages=56, 58}}</ref> as graphical capabilities consisted of simple geometry such as dots, lines or blocks that would occupy only a single screen.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=gJwoAQAAMAAJ&q=pong+basic+graphics|title=Graphics Programming with JFC|last1=Wall|first1=David|last2=Griffith|first2=Arthur|date=1999|publisher=Wiley|isbn=9780471283072|language=en|access-date=February 26, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190227060352/https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=gJwoAQAAMAAJ&q=pong+basic+graphics&dq=pong+basic+graphics&hl=en&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwjmxq2NudrgAhUEonEKHQivB24Q6AEINDAC|archive-date=February 27, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> First generation consoles were not capable of displaying more than two colours until later in the generation, and audio capabilities were limited with some consoles having no sound at all.

In 1972, two major developments influenced the future of the home video game market. In June, [[Nolan Bushnell]] and [[Ted Dabney]] founded [[Atari, Inc.|Atari]], which would go on to be one of the most well-known video game companies and play a vital role in the early generations of consoles.  In September, [[Magnavox]], an established electronics company, released the [[Magnavox Odyssey|Odyssey]]. Inspired by the Odyssey's ping-pong game, Atari would soon go on to market the game ''[[Pong]]'' in both arcade and home versions; [[Nintendo]], a well-established Japanese company that made a number of different products including but not limited to playing cards and toys, entered the video game console market for the first time in 1977 with its [[Color TV-Game series]].<ref name="powerplay">{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=U3u7AAAAIAAJ&q=%22color+tv+game%22&pg=PA180|title=Powerplay|last=Fleming|first=Dan|publisher=[[Manchester University Press]] ND|year=1996|isbn=978-0-7190-4717-6|page=180|access-date=February 26, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190511051413/https://books.google.com/books?id=U3u7AAAAIAAJ&pg=PA180&dq=%22color+tv+game%22|archive-date=May 11, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref>

== Overview ==
=== History ===
In 1951, [[Ralph Baer]] conceived the idea of an [[interactive television]] while designing a television set for [[Loral Corporation|Loral]] in [[The Bronx|the Bronx, New York]].<ref name="Rowman and Littlefield Publishers">{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=2ZD1AAAAQBAJ|title=Virtual Ascendance: Video Games and the Remaking of Reality|last1=Griffiths|first1=Devin C.|date=2013|publisher=Rowman and Littlefield Publishers|isbn=9781442216952|location=[[Lanham, Maryland]]|pages=14–15}}</ref> Baer did not pursue the idea, but it returned to him in August 1966 when he was the Chief Engineer and manager of the Equipment Design Division at [[Sanders Associates]]. By December 1966, he and a technician created a prototype that allowed a player to move a line across the screen. After a demonstration to the company's director of research and development, some funding was allotted and the project was made official. Baer spent the next few months designing further prototypes, and in February 1967 assigned technician Bill Harrison to begin building the project.<ref name="baer2005">{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=R9kTAQAAIAAJ|title=Videogames: in the beginning|last=Baer|first=Ralph H.|date=April 26, 2005|publisher=Rolenta Press|isbn=9780964384811|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134118/https://books.google.com/books?id=R9kTAQAAIAAJ|url-status=live |pages=30, 45}}</ref> Harrison spent the next few months in between other projects building out successive modifications to the prototype. Baer, meanwhile, collaborated with engineer Bill Rusch on the design of the console, including developing the basis of many games for the system. By May, the first game was developed and by June, multiple games were completed for what was then a second prototype box. This included a game where players controlled dots chasing each other and a light gun shooter game with a plastic rifle. By August 1967, Baer and Harrison had completed a third prototype machine, but Baer felt that he was not proving successful at designing fun games for the system; to make up for this he added Bill Rusch, who had helped him come up with the initial games for the console, to the project.<ref name="baer2005" /> He soon proved his value to the team by coming up with a way to display three dots on the screen at once rather than the previous two, and proposing the development of a ping pong game.<ref name="Replay">{{cite book |title=Replay: The History of Video Games |last=Donovan |first=Tristan |publisher=Yellow Ant |date=April 20, 2010 |isbn=978-0-9565072-0-4|title-link=Replay: The History of Video Games |pages=10–26}}</ref>

[[File:Ralph Baer's Brown Box prototype.jpg|thumb|right|The "Brown Box" prototype is the forerunner of the [[Magnavox Odyssey]], the first commercial home video game console.]]
[[File:First_cartridge_of_magnavox_odyssey.jpg|thumb|First cartridge of Magnavox Odyssey]]
As Sanders was a military contractor and not in the business of making and selling commercial electronics, the team approached several cable television industry companies to produce the console, but were unable to find a buyer. By January 1969 the team had produced the seventh prototype, nicknamed the "[[Brown Box]]".<ref name=":1" />{{Rp|12}} After a Sanders patent attorney recommended approaching television manufacturers, they found interest first at [[RCA Corporation|RCA]] and finally at [[Magnavox]], who entered negotiations in July 1969 and signed an agreement in January 1971.<ref name="Replay"/><ref name="HighScore">{{cite book |title=High Score! The Illustrated History of Electronic Games |last1=DeMaria |first1=Rusel |last2=Wilson |first2=Johnny L. |publisher=[[McGraw-Hill Education|McGraw Hill/Osborne]] |edition=2nd |date=December 2003 |isbn=978-0-07-223172-4 |page=18}}</ref> Magnavox designed the exterior of the machine, and re-engineered some of the internals with consultation from Baer and Harrison; they removed the ability to display color, reduced the number of controller types, and changed the system of selecting games from a dial to separate [[Cartridge (game card)|game cards]] that modified the console's circuitry when plugged into the console. Magnavox named the console the [[Magnavox Odyssey]] and announced the system's launch date for September 1972.<ref name="Replay"/><ref name="NGen23">{{cite magazine |title=The Great Videogame Swindle? |magazine=[[Next Generation (magazine)|Next Generation]] |issue=23 |publisher=[[Imagine Media]] |date=November 1996 |pages=67–68 |issn=1078-9693}}</ref>

In the late 1960s, [[Nolan Bushnell]] saw ''[[Spacewar!]]'' at Stanford University. ''Spacewar!'' is a 1962 [[early mainframe games|mainframe game]] developed by a group of students and employees at the [[Massachusetts Institute of Technology]]. Bushnell had worked at an amusement park, and felt that an [[arcade game]] version of the game would be very popular.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=qi-ItIG6QLwC&q=%22spacewar%21%22&pg=RA2-PA27|title=The Internet: A Historical Encyclopedia. Chronology. Volume 3|last1=Moschovitis|first1=Christos J. P.|last2=Poole (Christos)|first2=Hilary and Moshovitis|date=2005|publisher=ABC-CLIO|isbn=9781851096596|pages=27|access-date=March 3, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190306043935/https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=qi-ItIG6QLwC&pg=RA2-PA27&dq=%22spacewar!%22&hl=en&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwjZh43OiufgAhVhqHEKHb-RBagQ6AEILzAB#v=onepage&q=%22spacewar!%22&f=false|archive-date=March 6, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> The high price of computers capable of running the game, however, meant that any such arcade game would not be economically feasible. By 1970, however, [[minicomputer]]s were beginning to come down in price.<ref name="Replay"/><ref name="Technol">{{cite web |url=http://www.technologizer.com/2011/12/11/computer-space-and-the-dawn-of-the-arcade-video-game/ |last=Edwards |first=Benj |title=Computer Space and the Dawn of the Arcade Video Game |date=December 11, 2011 |work=[[Technologizer]] |access-date=April 13, 2016 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160322174236/http://www.technologizer.com/2011/12/11/computer-space-and-the-dawn-of-the-arcade-video-game/ |archive-date=March 22, 2016 |url-status=live}}</ref> He and his office mate, [[Ted Dabney]], agreed to work together to try and design a prototype of the game.<ref name="Atariinc">{{cite book |title=Atari Inc.: Business Is Fun |last1=Goldberg |first1=Marty |last2=Vendel |first2=Curt |publisher=Syzygy Press |date=November 25, 2012 |isbn=978-0-9855974-0-5 |pages=[https://archive.org/details/atariincbusiness0000gold/page/20 20–31] |url=https://archive.org/details/atariincbusiness0000gold/page/20 }}</ref> By the end of November 1970, the pair had abandoned the project as untenable, as economically feasible computers were not powerful enough. Dabney soon thought of a way to manipulate the video signal on the screen without a computer controlling it, and from there Syzygy Engineering came up with the idea of removing the computer altogether and building specialized hardware to handle everything for the game instead.<ref name="Replay"/><ref name="Technol"/> ''[[Computer Space]]'', the first commercial arcade video game, was released by the pair as Syzygy Engineering through [[Nutting Associates]] at the end of 1971 and after its release they incorporated as Atari in the following year and began designing more games. Bushnell saw a demonstration of the Odyssey console playing its ''Table Tennis'' game in early 1972 and assigned their first employee, [[Allan Alcorn]], to produce an arcade table tennis game. The result, ''[[Pong]]'', was the first major arcade video game success, and inspired a large number of arcade and [[dedicated console]] versions and clones, including Atari's Home Pong in 1975.<ref name="Replay"/>

=== Technology ===
The first generation of consoles did not contain a [[microprocessor]] and were based on custom codeless [[Finite-state machine|state machine computers]] consisting of [[Logic gate|discrete logic]] ([[Transistor–transistor logic|TTL]]) circuits comprising each element of the game itself. Over the generation, technology steadily improved and later consoles of the generation moved the bulk of the circuitry to custom [[integrated circuits]] such as Atari's custom Pong chips and General Instruments' [[AY-3-8500]] series.<ref name="vgc2">{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=vpjpAgAAQBAJ|title=Vintage Game Consoles: An Inside Look at Apple, Atari, Commodore, Nintendo, and the Greatest Gaming Platforms of All Time|last1=Loguidice|first1=Bill|last2=Barton|first2=Matt|date=February 24, 2014|publisher=CRC Press|isbn=9781135006501|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134053/https://books.google.com/books?id=vpjpAgAAQBAJ|url-status=live}}</ref>{{rp|119}}

Graphical capabilities were limited throughout the generation, often supported with physical accessories and screen overlays, but saw some improvement towards the end of the generation. While the Odyssey could only display 3 square dots in black and white, as the generation progressed, consoles started being able to display color as well as more complex shapes and text.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=QnBqDwAAQBAJ|title=CompTIA IT Fundamentals (ITF+) Study Guide: Exam FC0-U61|last=Docter|first=Quentin|date=August 17, 2018|publisher=John Wiley & Electrotennis required players to keep track of scores manually but later, many introduced score counters on the display to assist players in score tracking.<ref>{{Cite === <span class="anchor" id="Video game crash of 1977"></span>Market saturation and the end of the generation ===
In 1976, General Instrument produced a [[AY-3-8500|series of affordable integrated chips]] that allowed companies to simplify console production and lower costs.<ref name=":6">{{Cite magazine|last1=Eimbinder|first1=Jerry|last2=Eimbinder|first2=Eric|date=October 1980|title=Electronic Games: Space-age Leisure Activity|url=https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-Poptronics/80s/1980/Poptronics-1980-10.pdf|magazine=Popular Electronics|page=55|volume=18|issue=4|access-date=September 24, 2019|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134108/https://worldradiohistory.com/Archive-Poptronics/80s/1980/Poptronics-1980-10.pdf|url-status=live}}</ref> Due to this, many companies had entered the home console market by the late 1970s.<ref name=":8">{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=deBFx7QAwsQC|title=Encyclopedia of Video Games: A-L|last=Wolf|first=Mark J. P.|date=2012|publisher=ABC-CLIO|isbn=9780313379369|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134105/https://books.google.com/books?id=deBFx7QAwsQC|url-status=live}}</ref>{{Rp|147}} A significant number released consoles that were essentially clones of Atari's Home Pong and many were poorly made and rushed to market, causing the home console market to saturate.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=k5bNBQAAQBAJ|title=CoCo: The Colorful History of Tandy's Underdog Computer|last1=Pitre|first1=Boisy G.|last2=Loguidice|first2=Bill|date=December 10, 2013|publisher=CRC Press|isbn=9781466592483|pages=11|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134110/https://books.google.com/books?id=k5bNBQAAQBAJ|url-status=live}}</ref> The demand for the chip was so high that General Instruments could not supply enough to satisfy all the orders it was receiving, causing problems for some smaller companies.<ref name=":6"/> Coleco received their order early on, allowing them to build up strong production capabilities and have success with their Telstar range.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://archive.org/details/bluebookofcanadi1980unse|url-access=registration|title=The Blue Book of Canadian Business 1980|publisher=Canadian Newspaper Services International|year=1980|isbn=0-9690116-7-9|pages=[https://archive.org/details/bluebookofcanadi1980unse/page/182 182]}}</ref>

The start of the second generation and the next major advancement in home console technology began in 1976 with the release of the [[Fairchild Channel F]].<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=UlByDwAAQBAJ|title=Bits and Pieces: A History of Chiptunes|last=McAlpine|first=Kenneth B.|date=November 15, 2018|publisher=Oxford University Press|isbn=9780190496098|pages=14|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134116/https://books.google.com/books?id=UlByDwAAQBAJ|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="vgTextbook">{{cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=IExnDwAAQBAJ|title=The Video Games Textbook: History • Business • Technology|last=Wardyga|first=Brian J.|date=August 3, 2018|publisher=[[CRC Press]]|isbn=9780815390916|access-date=October 30, 2019|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134054/https://books.google.com/books?id=IExnDwAAQBAJ|url-status=live}}</ref>{{Rp|116}} The technology behind the first generation quickly became obsolete as consumers had the ability to purchase new games for second generation consoles instead of having to purchase new systems when they wanted new content as with the dedicated consoles of the first generation.<ref>{{Cite magazine|last=Salsberg|first=Arthur|date=September 1977|title=TV Electronic Games Grow Up|url=https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-Poptronics/70s/1977/Poptronics-1977-09.pdf|magazine=Popular Electronics|volume=12|issue=3|page=4|access-date=September 24, 2019|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134108/https://worldradiohistory.com/Archive-Poptronics/70s/1977/Poptronics-1977-09.pdf|url-status=live}}</ref> As people transitioned to the newer systems, some companies were left with surplus stock and were selling at a loss. The combination of market saturation and the start of the second generation caused many companies to leave the market completely.<ref name=":1" />{{Rp|22}}<ref name=":6" /> Sales of second generation consoles were only modest for the next few years until the arrival of the [[killer app]], the home port of ''[[Space Invaders]]'' for the Atari VCS in 1980.<ref>{{cite book |title=[[Ultimate History of Video Games]] |first=Steven |last=Kent | authorlink = Steven L. Kent |page=190 |publisher=[[Three Rivers Press]] |isbn=0-7615-3643-4 |year=2001}}</ref><ref>{{cite book |last=Weiss |first=Brett |title=Classic home video games, 1972–1984: a complete reference guide |year=2007 |publisher=[[McFarland & Company|McFarland]] |location=Jefferson, N.C. |isbn=978-0-7864-3226-4 |page=108}}</ref>

There were hundreds of home video game consoles known to have existed in the first generation of video games.<ref name=pong-clones>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=fU-fDwAAQBAJ|title=Vintage Games 2.0: An Insider Look at the Most Influential Games of All Time|last=Barton|first=Matt|date=May 8, 2019|publisher=CRC Press|isbn=9781000000924|pages=18|language=en|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134120/https://books.google.com/books?id=fU-fDwAAQBAJ|url-status=live}}</ref> This section lists the most notable.

=== Odyssey series ===
{{Main|Odyssey series}}
In September 1972, Magnavox released the world's first home video game console, the Magnavox Odyssey.<ref name="Wolf2008">{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=XiM0ntMybNwC|title=The Video Game Explosion: A History from PONG to Playstation and Beyond|last=Wolf|first=Mark J. P.|date=2008|publisher=[[ABC-CLIO]]|isbn=9780313338687|language=en|access-date=March 1, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190302152453/https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=XiM0ntMybNwC&pg=PA359&dq=magnavox+odyssey&hl=en&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwiU0NW67uHgAhWKqaQKHdVzA_oQ6AEIMDAB#v=onepage&q=magnavox%20odyssey&f=false|archive-date=March 2, 2019|url-status=live |pages=50, 55}}</ref> It came packaged with [[board game]] paraphernalia such as cards, paper money and dice to enhance the games.<ref name="Wolf2008"/> It had features that became industry standard in subsequent generations such as detachable controllers, light gun accessories and interchangeable game media.<ref name="vgc2" />{{Rp|xvii}} While no game data was stored on the circuit cards as they would be in future consoles, they could be used to select one of the twelve games built onto the hardware. Magnavox licensed its video game patents to other companies for a fee and prosecuted companies who released consoles without a licensing agreement.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=5Wx6-uv-DSkC&pg=PA119|title=Invisible Engines: How Software Platforms Drive Innovation and Transform Industries|last1=Evans|first1=David S.|last2=Hagiu|first2=Andrei|last3=Schmalensee|first3=Richard|date=February 15, 2008|publisher=[[MIT Press]]|isbn=9780262262644|pages=119|language=en|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134054/https://books.google.com/books?id=5Wx6-uv-DSkC&pg=PA119|url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="Magnavox Sues" />

It was with the Odyssey that Nintendo first became involved in the home video game market. According to Martin Picard in the ''International Journal of Computer Game Research'': "in 1971, Nintendo had – even before the marketing of the first home console in the United States – an alliance with the American pioneer Magnavox to develop and produce [[Light gun|optoelectronic guns]] for the Odyssey, since it was similar to what Nintendo was able to offer in the Japanese toy market in [the] 1970s."<ref name="picard">{{cite web|url=http://gamestudies.org/1302/articles/picard|title=The Foundation of Geemu: A Brief History of Early Japanese video games|last=Picard|first=Martin|publisher=International Journal of Computer Game Research|date=June 24, 2015|access-date=September 16, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150624050100/http://gamestudies.org/1302/articles/picard|archive-date=June 24, 2015|url-status=live}}</ref>

In 1974 [[North American Philips]] purchased Magnavox and released a series of eight Odyssey consoles in North America from 1975 to 1977. All of them were [[dedicated console]]s, and each subsequent release was an improvement over the previous, adding features such as additional game variations, on-screen displays, and player-controlled handicaps such as smaller paddle sizes and variable ball speed.<ref name="Wolf2008"/><ref>{{Cite news|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=auMDAAAAMBAJ|title=Popular Mechanics|last=Morgan|first=Cindy|journal=Popular Mechanics |date=October 1976|publisher=[[Hearst Magazines]]|issn=0032-4558|volume=146|issue=4|pages=80|language=en}}</ref> Three Odyssey series consoles were also released in Europe with similar features from 1976 to 1978.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.voxodyssey.com/odyssey-2100/|title=Philips Odyssey 2100|website=Vox Odyssey|language=en|access-date=November 26, 2019|archive-date=December 13, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191213191814/https://www.voxodyssey.com/odyssey-2100/|url-status=live}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.voxodyssey.com/odyssey-2001/|title=Philips Odyssey 2001|website=Vox Odyssey|language=en|access-date=November 26, 2019|archive-date=December 11, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20191211082548/https://www.voxodyssey.com/odyssey-2001/|url-status=live}}</ref>

=== TV Tennis Electrotennis ===
{{Main|TV Tennis Electrotennis}}
On September 12, 1975, several months before the release of ''Home Pong'' in North America, [[Epoch Co.|Epoch]] released Japan's first home console, the TV Tennis Electrotennis. The technology was licensed from Magnavox and it contained a single ball and paddle style game that resembled Pong but without an onscreen score display.<ref name=":5" /> The game controls were contained within the base unit and it connected to a television set through an [[ultra high frequency]] (UHF) antenna, as opposed to being directly connected, which was unique to the console at the time.<ref name="picard" /> Compared to popular consoles of the generation, it performed poorly with an approximate 20,000 units sold.<ref name=":5"/>

=== Atari Home Pong ===
{{Main|Pong#Home version}}
In late 1975 Atari released a home version of their popular arcade game ''Pong''.<ref name="Ultimate-Home1">{{cite book|title=Ultimate History of Video Games|last=Kent|first=Steven|publisher=Three Rivers Press|year=2001|isbn=0-7615-3643-4|pages=80–83|chapter=Could You Repeat That Two More Times?}}</ref> It had been in development since 1974 under the lead of [[Allan Alcorn]] and Harold Lee.<ref name=":1">{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=YVTNBQAAQBAJ|title=The Golden Age of Video Games: The Birth of a Multibillion Dollar Industry|last=Dillon|first=Roberto|date=April 19, 2016|publisher=CRC Press|isbn=9781439873243|language=en|access-date=March 1, 2019|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190302090641/https://books.google.co.uk/books?id=YVTNBQAAQBAJ&pg=PA21&dq=color+tv-game&hl=en&sa=X&ved=0ahUKEwjxpODk8OHgAhXDy6QKHavdCc8Q6AEIRDAF#v=onepage&q=color%20tv-game&f=false|archive-date=March 2, 2019|url-status=live}}</ref> By the end of 1975, Atari had become a major company in the home console market due to ''Home Pong''.<ref name=":2">{{Cite web|url=https://www.gamasutra.com/view/feature/3900/the_history_of_pong_avoid_missing_.php?print=1|title=The History Of Pong: Avoid Missing Game to Start Industry|last1=Barton|first1=Matt|last2=Loguidice|first2=Bill|date=January 9, 2009|website=Gamasutra|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190119122802/https://www.gamasutra.com/view/feature/3900/the_history_of_pong_avoid_missing_.php?print=1|archive-date=January 19, 2019|url-status=dead|access-date=February 26, 2019}}</ref> Following Pong's success, Magnavox filed suit against Atari for infringement on its technology patents and ended up settling out of court with Atari becoming a licensee of Magnavox.<ref name="Magnavox Sues">{{cite news|title=Magnavox Sues Firms Making Video Games, Charges Infringement|date=April 17, 1974|newspaper=[[The Wall Street Journal]]|pages=15}}</ref>

Home video games achieved widespread popularity with the release of a home version of ''Pong'' and its success sparked hundreds of clones, including the [[Telstar (game console)|Coleco Telstar]], which went on to be a success in its own right with over a dozen models.

=== Coleco Telstar series ===
{{Main|Coleco Telstar series}}
Starting in 1976, [[Coleco]] released a series of fourteen dedicated consoles up until 1978,<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=8kFqDwAAQBAJ&q=coleco+telstar+classic+launch+price|url-access=subscription|title=Hey! All of the handheld systems from the first generation are [[dedicated console]]s and started late into the first generation. It was not until the second generation and the release of the [[Microvision]] that players could purchase games separately for the systems.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=ercrDwAAQBAJ|title=The Game Console: A Photographic History from Atari to Xbox|last=Amos|first=Evan|date=November 6, 2018|publisher=No Starch Press|isbn=9781593277727|language=en|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134108/https://books.google.com/books?id=ercrDwAAQBAJ|url-status=live}}</ref>{{Rp|46}} The early dedicated handheld consoles were eventually eclipsed in popularity by programmable video games, which became popular in the [[Fourth generation of video game consoles|fourth generation]] with the introduction of the [[Game Boy]].<ref name=":8" />{{Rp|316}}

One notable example is the [[Mattel]] handheld game series, which were released from 1976 to 1982. The first to be released were ''[[Mattel Auto Race]]'' and ''Mattel Football,'' while some of the latter models like the ''Mattel Speed Freak'' and the ''Mattel Competition Football'' handhelds are closer to the end of the first generation (1982)<ref>{{Cite web |title=Mattel Handheld Games |url=https://www.handheldmuseum.com/Mattel/ |access-date=2023-08-12 |website=www.handheldmuseum.com}}</ref>''.'' They were followed by other titles based on sports and some licensed properties such as ''[[Battlestar Galactica]]''. Each game had basic controls, a simple LED interface and a buzzer for sound.<ref name="vgc2"/>{{rp|70}} The series was popular, sold well and, at times, was difficult to find due to high demand.<ref>{{cite news|url=https://news.google.com/newspapers?nid=1499&dat=19780529&id=aG8aAAAAIBAJ&pg=2458,6830499|title=Football Calculator Scoring Well|last=Armstrong|first=Douglas D.|date=May 29, 1978|work=[[The Milwaukee Journal]]|access-date=July 17, 2016|page=1|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160522082201/https://news.google.com/newspapers?nid=1499&dat=19780529&id=aG8aAAAAIBAJ&sjid=jykEAAAAIBAJ&pg=2458,6830499|archive-date=May 22, 2016|url-status=live}}</ref>

In the same year, Coleco began to release handheld consoles after the end of the Telstar home console series.<ref name="vgc2" />{{Rp|121}} They released ''[[Electronic Quarterback]]'', which expanded on the popular American football style games by adding new features.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=ny-CAAAAMAAJ|title=The First Quarter: A 25-Year History of Video Games|last=Kent|first=Steven L.|date=2000|publisher=BWD Press|isbn=978-0-9704755-0-3|pages=166|language=en|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=January 17, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230117222935/https://books.google.com/books?id=ny-CAAAAMAAJ|url-status=live}}</ref> Alongside ''Mattel Football'', it became the other popular sports game of the period.<ref>{{Cite book|url=https://books.google.com/books?id=tfTXAQAAQBAJ|title=American Sports: A History of Icons, Idols, and Ideas [4 volumes]: A History of Icons, Idols, and Ideas|last=Nelson|first=Murry R.|date=May 23, 2013|publisher=ABC-CLIO|isbn=978-0-313-39753-0|pages=1416|language=en|access-date=September 23, 2020|archive-date=February 9, 2023|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230209134127/https://books.google.com/books?id=tfTXAQAAQBAJ|url-status=live}}</ref>
