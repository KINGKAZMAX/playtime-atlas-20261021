The {{nihongo foot|'''Nintendo GameCube'''|ニンテンドーゲームキューブ|Nintendō Gēmukyūbu|lead=yes|group=lower-alpha}}, commonly known as the '''GameCube''',{{efn|Abbreviated in Japan as '''GC''' or '''NGC''' and in North America and Europe as '''GCN'''.}} is a [[home video game console]] developed and marketed by [[Nintendo]]. It was released in Japan on September 14, 2001, in North America on November 18, 2001, in Europe on May 3, 2002, and in Australia on May 17, 2002. Serving as the successor to the [[Nintendo 64]], the system competed against [[Sony Interactive Entertainment|Sony]]'s [[PlayStation 2]], [[Microsoft]]'s [[Xbox (console)|Xbox]], and [[Sega]]'s [[Dreamcast]] as part of the [[sixth generation of video game consoles]].

Nintendo began designing the console in 1998 in partnership with [[ArtX]] for the system logic and [[graphics processing unit]], [[IBM]] for the central processor, and [[Panasonic|Matsushita Electric Industrial]] for the optical drive. The GameCube is the first Nintendo console to use [[Nintendo optical discs|optical discs]] as its primary storage medium instead of [[ROM cartridge]]s, relying on proprietary 8{{nbsp}}cm [[MiniDVD]]-based discs and external [[GameCube accessories#Memory cards|memory cards]] for [[saved game]] data. Unlike its primary sixth-generation competitors, the standard GameCube was designed exclusively for playing games and cannot play standard [[DVD]] movies or [[CD]] audio, although Matsushita released the [[Panasonic Q]] hybrid DVD model in Japan. The system introduced a handlebar-style [[GameCube controller]] with staggered [[analog stick]]s and pressure-sensitive triggers. [[GameCube accessories]] include the [[WaveBird Wireless Controller]], the [[GameCube – Game Boy Advance link cable]] for connecting a [[Game Boy Advance]] (GBA) as a secondary screen or controller, the [[Game Boy Player]] attachment for playing handheld cartridges on a television, and [[GameCube online functionality|broadband and modem adapters]] that enabled network play in a small number of titles.

The GameCube received positive reviews for its ergonomic controller, compact hardware, and exclusive software library, alongside criticism for its toy-like exterior and lack of multimedia capabilities and built-in online infrastructure. Although the hardware and software business remained profitable for Nintendo, lifetime sales reached 21.74{{nbsp}}million units worldwide, falling well short of the PlayStation 2 and trailing the Xbox while outselling the Dreamcast. Analysts attributed its third-place finish to a limited initial software lineup, weak [[third-party developer|third-party]] multiplatform support, the absence of DVD playback, and a brand image that skewed toward younger players during a period when older demographics drove industry growth.

Nintendo succeeded the GameCube with the [[Wii]] in November 2006 and discontinued the console in February 2007. Early Wii models provide native [[backward compatibility]] with GameCube software, memory cards, and controllers. Retrospectively, critics have praised the GameCube for debuting major Nintendo franchises including ''[[Pikmin]]'', ''[[Luigi's Mansion]]'', ''[[Animal Crossing]]'' outside Japan, and the ''[[Metroid Prime]]'' subseries, as well as hosting acclaimed titles such as ''[[Super Smash Bros. Melee]]'' (2001), ''[[Super Mario Sunshine]]'' (2002), ''[[The Legend of Zelda: The Wind Waker]]'' (2002), and ''[[Resident Evil 4]]'' (2005). The GameCube controller has remained in production through official adapters and wireless reissues across the Wii, [[Wii U]], [[Nintendo Switch]], and [[Nintendo Switch 2]].

===Release===
The GameCube launched in Japan on September 14, 2001, with approximately 500,000 consoles shipped to retailers alongside three launch games: ''Luigi's Mansion'', ''[[Wave Race: Blue Storm]]'', and ''[[Super Monkey Ball (video game)|Super Monkey Ball]]''.<ref name="Launch – BBC">{{Cite news |date=September 14, 2001 |title=GameCube Launches in Japan |work=[[BBC News]] |url=https://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/entertainment/1543848.stm |url-status=live |access-date=July 8, 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150117054316/http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/entertainment/1543848.stm |archive-date=January 17, 2015}}</ref><ref name="Japan launch – GS">{{Cite web |last=Sato |first=Yukiyoshi Ike |date=September 13, 2001 |title=Nintendo GameCube Launches in Japan |url=http://www.gamespot.com/news/nintendo-gamecube-launches-in-japan-2812298 |access-date=July 8, 2013 |website=[[GameSpot]]}}</ref> Nintendo originally scheduled the North American launch for November 5, 2001, but pushed the date back nearly two weeks to November 18 so factories could supply over 700,000 units alongside twelve launch titles.<ref name="Record launch – CNET" /><ref name="US launch delay">{{Cite web |last=Becker |first=David |date=August 23, 2001 |title=Nintendo Delays U.S. Launch of GameCube |url=http://news.cnet.com/Nintendo-delays-U.S.-launch-of-GameCube/2100-1040_3-272053.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150402105211/http://news.cnet.com/Nintendo-delays-U.S.-launch-of-GameCube/2100-1040_3-272053.html |archive-date=April 2, 2015 |access-date=July 8, 2013 |website=CNET}}</ref>

Nintendo technical director Howard Cheng stated that the company's primary engineering goal was to adopt a clean [[RISC]] architecture that would minimize development bottlenecks and keep hardware costs low.<ref name="It's Alive" /> ArtX vice president Greg Buchner noted that the hardware team spent three years consulting with Nintendo of America and software studios to design a developer-centric architecture that delivered high performance without requiring complex programming workarounds.<ref name="ATI discusses GC graphics" /><ref>{{Cite web |title=Gaming Intelligence Agency – Interview with Greg Buchner |url=http://thegia.com/archive/features/f010606.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181125115721/http://thegia.com/archive/features/f010606.html |archive-date=November 25, 2018 |access-date=November 25, 2018 |website=The Gaming Intelligence Agency}}</ref>

The console's central processing unit is the [[Gekko (processor)|Gekko]], a custom 32-bit [[IBM]] [[PowerPC]] microprocessor based on the [[PowerPC 750CXe]] and fabricated on a 180{{nbsp}}nm CMOS process with [[copper interconnects]].<ref name="Nintendo press conference May 1999" /><ref name="Specs – Anandtech">{{Cite web |last=Shimpi |first=Anand Lal |date=December 7, 2001 |title=Hardware Behind the Consoles – Part II: Nintendo's GameCube |url=http://www.anandtech.com/show/858 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130723162237/http://www.anandtech.com/show/858 |archive-date=July 23, 2013 |access-date=July 9, 2013 |publisher=[[AnandTech]]}}</ref> Running at 486{{nbsp}}MHz (1,125{{nbsp}}[[Instructions per second|DMIPS]]), Gekko contains 64{{nbsp}}KB of L1 cache (32{{nbsp}}KB instruction and 32{{nbsp}}KB data, half of which can be locked as a fast scratchpad for [[direct memory access]]) and 256{{nbsp}}KB of on-die L2 cache.<ref name="Specs – Anandtech" /> Its 64-bit [[floating-point unit]] incorporates roughly 50 custom [[SIMD]] "paired singles" instructions tailored for 3D geometry and lighting calculations, yielding a CPU floating-point throughput of 1.9{{nbsp}}[[FLOPS|GFLOPS]] and a system-wide peak of 10.5{{nbsp}}GFLOPS.<ref name="Specs – Anandtech" /><ref name="GC TD NUK">{{Cite web |title=Technical Details |url=https://www.nintendo.co.uk/Corporate/Nintendo-History/Nintendo-GameCube/Technical-Details/Technical-Details-627134.html |access-date=November 7, 2020 |publisher=[[Nintendo of Europe]] |archive-date=July 5, 2022 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20220705184723/https://www.nintendo.co.uk/Corporate/Nintendo-History/Nintendo-GameCube/Technical-Details/Technical-Details-627134.html |url-status=live}}</ref><ref name="Specs – ExtremeTech">{{Cite news |last=Hackman |first=Mark |date=June 12, 2001 |title=Nintendo GameCube Unwrapped |work=[[ExtremeTech]] |url=http://www.extremetech.com/extreme/49118-nintendo-gamecube-unwrapped |url-status=live |access-date=July 9, 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131203141509/http://www.extremetech.com/extreme/49118-nintendo-gamecube-unwrapped |archive-date=December 3, 2013}}</ref> Gekko communicates with the system's northbridge over a 64-bit, 162{{nbsp}}MHz [[Front-side bus|front-side bus]] with a peak bandwidth of 1.3{{nbsp}}GB/s.<ref name="Specs – Anandtech" />

The ArtX-designed ATI "Flipper" [[application-specific integrated circuit|ASIC]], clocked at 162{{nbsp}}MHz on an 180{{nbsp}}nm [[NEC]] [[eDRAM]] process, integrates the graphics processor, northbridge, I/O controller, and audio processor on a single die.<ref name="Specs – Anandtech" /><ref name="Specs – PC World">{{Cite magazine |last=Gray |first=Douglas F. |date=June 14, 2001 2013}}</ref> Flipper contains 3{{nbsp}}MB of embedded [[MoSys]] [[1T-SRAM]] split between a 2{{nbsp}}MB [[framebuffer]]/[[Z-buffering|Z-buffer]] (7.8{{nbsp}}GB/s bandwidth) and a 1{{nbsp}}MB [[Texture memory|texture cache]] (10.4{{nbsp}}GB/s bandwidth), both operating at latencies under 6.2{{nbsp}}nanoseconds.<ref name="Specs – Anandtech" /><ref name="NintendoGCNspecs">{{Cite web |access-date=March 28, 2008 |url=http://register.nintendo.com/techspecgcn |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080502201300/http://register.nintendo.com/techspecgcn |archive-date=May 2, 2008 |title=GCN Technical 24{{nbsp}}MB of external 1T-SRAM main system memory running at 324{{nbsp}}MHz across a 64-bit bus (2.6{{nbsp}}GB/s bandwidth) and 16{{nbsp}}MB of 81{{nbsp}}MHz [[DRAM]] serving as an auxiliary buffer for audio and optical drive data.<ref name="Specs – Anandtech" /><ref name="NintendoGCNspecs" /> Flipper integrates a custom 81{{nbsp}}MHz [[Macronix]] 16-bit [[digital signal processor]] capable of ===Storage and memory cards===
[[File:Nintendo GameCube memory card.png|thumb|right|upright|Memory Card 59]]
Following earlier cartridge-complementing magnetic and optical experiments such as the [[Famicom Disk System]], [[SNES-CD]], and [[64DD]], the GameCube became Nintendo's first console to rely exclusively on optical discs for primary retail distribution.<ref>{{Cite web |last=Bonsor |first=Kevin |date=December 4, 2000 |title=How GameCube Works |url=http://electronics.howstuffworks.com/gamecube2.htm |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140106041121/http://electronics.howstuffworks.com/gamecube2.htm |archive-date=January 6, 2014 |access-date=July 8, 2013 |publisher=[[HowStuffWorks]]}}</ref> The proprietary 8{{nbsp}}cm [[GameCube Game Disc]], developed by Matsushita based on the [[MiniDVD]] standard, operates in [[constant angular velocity]] mode at transfer rates of 16 to 25{{nbsp}}Mbit/s and holds up to 1.5{{nbsp}}GB of data.<ref name="miniDVD – IGN">{{Cite web |date=August 23, 2000 |title=GameCube: A Digital Wonder |url=https://www.ign.com/articles/2000/08/24/gamecube-a-digital-wonder |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200625172833/https://www.ign.com/articles/2000/08/24/gamecube-a-digital-wonder |archive-date=June 25, 2020 |access-date=July 8, 2013 |website=[[IGN]]}}</ref><ref name="Specs – Anandtech" /> Nintendo chose the smaller format to combat software piracy through a proprietary copy-protection scheme rather than the standard [[Content Scramble System]] used on commercial DVDs, to avoid paying DVD Forum licensing fees for movie playback, and to reduce disc access times.<ref name="Matsushita" /><ref name="miniDVD – IGN" /> While 1.5{{nbsp}}GB accommodated most software, certain content-heavy games required two discs, higher video compression, or trimmed audio compared to the 4.7{{nbsp}}GB single-layer and 8.5{{nbsp}}GB dual-layer standard DVDs used by the PlayStation 2 and Xbox.<ref name="miniDVD – IGN" />

Because the console contains no internal non-volatile storage for game saves, players must insert a proprietary [[GameCube accessories#Memory cards|Memory Card]] into one of the two front memory card slots to record progress, with save files measured in 8{{nbsp}}KB "blocks".<ref name="Memory card issues">{{Cite web |title=Nintendo GameCube Memory Card 1019 |url=https://www.nintendo.com/consumer/memorycard1019.jsp |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130702181148/http://www.nintendo.com/consumer/memorycard1019.jsp |archive-date=July 2, 2013 |access-date=July 12, 2013 |publisher=[[Nintendo]]}}</ref> Nintendo released three official capacities coded by casing color: the gray Memory Card 59 (512{{nbsp}}KB or 4{{nbsp}}Mb), the black Memory Card 251 (2{{nbsp}}MB or 16{{nbsp}}Mb), and the white Memory Card 1019 (8{{nbsp}}MB or 64{{nbsp}}Mb), alongside higher-capacity cards manufactured by third-party companies.<ref name="Memory card issues" /> Official GameCube game cases include a molded interior clip above the disc hub to store a memory card.

The GameCube is the first Nintendo home console to include a built-in [[graphical user interface|system menu]] and an internal battery-backed [[real-time clock]].<ref name="Manual101" /> Players access the menu by powering on the console without a Game Disc inserted or by holding down the A button on Controller 1 during boot-up.<ref name="Manual101" /> The menu presents a rotating 3D cube whose faces are selected by tilting the analog stick in four directions: tilting up boots the inserted game, tilting right accesses the internal date and time settings, tilting down opens the Memory Card manager to view, copy, move, or erase save data blocks, and tilting left opens the Options screen for mono or stereo audio, screen position adjustment, and system language.<ref name="Manual101" /> The boot sequence includes two hidden audio [[Easter egg (media)|Easter eggs]] triggered by holding down the Z button on Controller 1 (replacing the startup xylophone chime with squeaks and a child's laugh) or on all four connected controllers simultaneously (playing a traditional Japanese percussion cadence).<ref name="Easter Eggs">{{Cite magazine |last=Taljonick |first=Ryan |date=April 3, 2013 |title=The 100 Best Easter Eggs of All Time |url=https://www.gamesradar.com/100-best-easter-eggs-all-time/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20130908223145/http://www.gamesradar.com/100-best-easter-eggs-all-time/ |archive-date=September 8, 2013 |access-date=July 16, 2013 |magazine=[[GamesRadar+]]}}</ref> Furthermore, when the ambient background music of the main system menu is sped up 16 times, it plays the startup fanfare of the 1986 [[Famicom Disk System]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=September 8, 2021 |title=Talking Point: Which Nintendo Console Has The Best Start-Up Sound? |url=https://www.nintendolife.com/features/talking-point-which-nintendo-console-has-the-best-start-up-sound |access-date=October 18, 2026 |website=[[Nintendo Life]]}}</ref>

===Panasonic Q===
[[File:Panasonic-Q-Console-Set.png|thumb|The Panasonic Q integrates a front-loading DVD player with GameCube hardware.]]
As part of Matsushita's May 1999 optical drive agreement with Nintendo, Matsushita developed the {{nihongo foot|'''Panasonic Q'''|パナソニックQ|Panasonikku Kyū|lead=yes|group=lower-alpha}} (model SL-GC10), an officially licensed hybrid system combining GameCube hardware with a commercial DVD and CD player.<ref name="Q Christmas – NWR">{{Cite news |last=Lake |first=Max |date=October 19, 2001 |title=Q for Christmas in Japan |work=Nintendo World Report |agency=[[Reuters]] |url=https://www.nintendoworldreport.com/news/6618/q-for-christmas-in-japan |url-status=live |access-date=March 29, 2021 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210125230928/https://www.nintendoworldreport.com/news/6618/q-for-christmas-in-japan |archive-date=January 25, 2021}}</ref> Encased in a mirrored [[stainless steel]] chassis with a front-loading motorized disc tray, a backlit LCD screen, optical digital audio output, and top-mounted playback controls, the Panasonic Q launched exclusively in Japan on December 14, 2001, at ¥39,800, after Panasonic confirmed in November 2001 that it had no plans for a North American release.<ref name="Q Christmas – NWR" /><ref name="PanasonicQ-IGN">{{Cite web |last=Mirabella III |first=Fran |date=April 9, 2002 |title=Panasonic Q Review |url=https://www.ign.com/articles/2002/04/09/panasonic-q-review |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190414182159/https://www.ign.com/articles/2002/04/09/panasonic-q-review |archive-date=April 14, 2019 |access-date=May 1, 2019 |website=[[IGN]]}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |date=November 2, 2001 |title=No Q for the U.S. |url=http://www.nintendoworldreport.com/news/6687/no-q-for-the-us |access-date=February 27, 2017 |website=Nintendo World Report}}</ref> Because its bottom feet obstructed the standard [[Game Boy Player]], Panasonic released a specialized Game Boy Player attachment (model SH-GB10-H) tailored to the Q's chassis. Despite a price cut in July 2002 following reductions on the standard GameCube, the Q's high price relative to buying a standard console and a standalone DVD player resulted in low sales, and Panasonic discontinued the unit in December 2003.<ref>{{Cite web |date=July 1, 2002 |title=Panasonic Drops Q Price |url=http://www.nintendoworldreport.com/news/7507/panasonic-drops-q-price |access-date=February 27, 2017 |website=Nintendo World Report}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Cole |first=Michael |date=December 17, 2003 |title=Panasonic Q Discontinued – News |url=http://www.nintendoworldreport.com/news/9283/panasonic-q-discontinued |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160304084816/http://www.nintendoworldreport.com/news/9283/panasonic-q-discontinued |archive-date=March 4, 2016 |access-date=July 28, 2015 |website=Nintendo World Report}}</ref>

===Technical specs===

===Online gaming===
{{Main|GameCube online functionality}}
[[File:GameCube online setup.png|thumb|right|A GameCube equipped with a Broadband Adapter and the Japan-exclusive [[ASCII Corporation|ASCII]] Keyboard Controller for ''[[Phantasy Star Online]]''.]]
Unlike Microsoft with [[Xbox Live]] and Sony with the [[PlayStation 2 Expansion Bay|PlayStation 2 Network Adaptor]], Nintendo remained skeptical of consumer demand for subscription online console gaming and did not establish a unified first-party online network for the GameCube.<ref name="GCN jumps online2" /> Instead, network connectivity required purchasing the official [[Nintendo GameCube Broadband Adapter and Modem Adapter|Broadband Adapter or Modem Adapter]], which were released in autumn 2002 and fit into Serial Port 1 on the underside of the console, while individual publishers managed their own servers.<ref name="GCN jumps online2" /><ref name="nwr" />

Only eight GameCube titles officially supported network functionality.<ref name="nwr" /><ref name="konami.jp-2005">{{Cite web |date=November 19, 2005 |title=ダウンロード |url=http://www.konami.jp:80/gs/game/pawa/10_gc/ |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20051119025520/http://www.konami.jp/gs/game/pawa/10_gc/ |archive-date=November 19, 2005 |access-date=November 25, 2017 |website=Konami}}</ref> In Western territories, Internet play was limited to three Sega role-playing games: ''[[Phantasy Star Online Episode I & II]]'', ''Phantasy Star Online Episode I & II Plus'', and ''[[Phantasy Star Online Episode III: C.A.R.D. Revolution]]'', whose official servers operated until 2007.<ref name="nwr" /> Japan received two additional Internet-enabled titles: [[Chunsoft]]'s cooperative role-playing game ''[[Homeland (video game)|Homeland]]'' (which allowed a player's console to act as a host server for up to 35 other players) and Konami's ''Jikkyō Powerful Pro Yakyū 10'', which supported downloadable roster updates.<ref name="nwr" /><ref name="konami.jp-2005" /> Three first-party racing games—''[[Mario Kart: Double Dash]]'', ''[[1080° Avalanche]]'', and ''[[Kirby Air Ride]]''—supported local area network (LAN) multiplayer across up to eight linked consoles using the Broadband Adapter, which enthusiasts also routed over the Internet using PC [[Tunneling protocol|tunneling]] software.<ref name="nwr" /><ref>{{Cite web |last=Thorsen |first=Tor |date=October 28, 2003 |title=DIY Developers Bring GameCube Online |url=https://www.gamespot.com/articles/diy-developers-bring-gamecube-online/1100-6077489/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171117122528/https://www.gamespot.com/articles/diy-developers-bring-gamecube-online/1100-6077489/ |archive-date=November 17, 2017 |access-date=November 27, 2017 |website=[[GameSpot]]}}</ref>

The [[GameCube controller]] has been officially supported across five generations of Nintendo home consoles, more than any other Nintendo gamepad. Following native controller ports on early Wii consoles (used in titles such as ''[[Super Smash Bros. Brawl]]'' and ''[[Mario Kart Wii]]''), Nintendo released a four-port USB GameCube Controller Adapter alongside [[Super Smash Bros. for Nintendo 3DS and Wii U|''Super Smash Bros. for Wii U'']] in 2014.<ref name="Controller circulation">{{Cite web |last=Perlmutter |first=Zachary |date=May 20, 2019 |title=How Nintendo Kept the GameCube Controller in Constant Circulation |url=https://www.nintendoenthusiast.com/2019/05/20/nintendo-gamecube-controller-constant-circulation/ |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20190602224752/https://www.nintendoenthusiast.com/2019/05/20/nintendo-gamecube-controller-constant-circulation/ |archive-date=June 2, 2019 |access-date=June 2, 2019 |website=Nintendo Enthusiast}}</ref> System software update 4.0.0 for the [[Nintendo Switch]] in October 2017 enabled the USB adapter to recognize original GameCube controllers across compatible Switch software and specifically in ''[[Super Smash Bros. Ultimate]]'' (2018), which was accompanied by a new production run of wired GameCube controllers.<ref name="Controller circulation" /> For the launch of the [[Nintendo Switch 2]] in June 2025, Nintendo maintained support for the USB adapter and released a new official wireless GameCube controller featuring a rechargeable battery, motion-sensing gyroscope controls, and extra system buttons designed for ''Nintendo GameCube – Nintendo Classics''.<ref name="NSO Switch 2" /><ref>{{Cite web |date=June 6, 2025 |title=Surprise! GameCube Controller for Nintendo Switch 2 Has Gyro Controls |url=https://www.ign.com/articles/surprise-gamecube-controller-for-nintendo-switch-2-has-gyro-controls-and-fans-are-having-fun-working-out-what-its-compatible-with |access-date=October 18, 2026 |website=[[IGN]]}}</ref>
