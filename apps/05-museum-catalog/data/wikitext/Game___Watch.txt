{{nihongo foot|'''Game & Watch'''| ゲーム&ウオッチ|Gēmu & Uotchi|lead=yes|group=lower-alpha}} is a series of [[handheld electronic game]]s developed by [[Nintendo]]. Designed by [[Gunpei Yokoi]], the first game, ''[[Ball (video game)|Ball]]'' was released in 1980 and the original production run of the devices continued until 1991. The name Game & Watch reflects their dual functionality: a single game paired with a [[digital clock]] on a [[segmented liquid-crystal display]] (LCD) screen. The Game & Watch series proved a monumental success, selling a total of 43.4 million units globally, marking Nintendo's first major worldwide success with [[Electronic game|electronic games]].

== History ==
The Game & Watch was the brainchild of designer [[Gunpei Yokoi]], the head of [[Nintendo Research & Development 1]] (R&D1), who was inspired during a trip on a [[Shinkansen]] high-speed train, where he noticed a bored businessman idly pressing buttons on a credit-card-sized pocket [[calculator]].<ref name="vice yokoi">{{Cite news |last=Alt |first=Matt |date=12 November 2020 |title=How Gunpei Yokoi Reinvented Nintendo |url=https://www.vice.com/en/article/how-gunpei-yokoi-reinvented-nintendo/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201113062629/https://www.vice.com/en/article/pkdbx7/how-gunpei-yokoi-reinvented-nintendo |archive-date=13 November 2020 |access-date=12 November 2020 |work=[[Vice (magazine)|Vice]]}}</ref> This sparked the idea of a compact, discreet toy for adults to pass the time on public transport. At the time, Nintendo was struggling financially, and R&D1 consisted of just Yokoi and his assistant, [[Satoru Okada]]. The two looked at other [[handheld electronic game]]s, including ''[[Mattel Auto Race]]'', the [[Microvision]], and ''[[Simon (game)|Simon]]'', but found these too large and reliant on low-resolution [[Vacuum fluorescent display|vacuum fluorescent]] or [[Light-emitting diode|LED]] displays that rendered abstract graphics.<ref name="Kurokawa 2022">{{Cite web |last=Kurokawa |first=Fumio |date=28 March 2022 |title=元任天堂・岡田 智氏の独立独歩 前編 "技術的に不可能"を覆したゲーム＆ウオッチ 「ビデオゲームの語り部たち」：第27部 |trans-title=Former Nintendo employee Satoshi Okada's independent journey Part 1: Game & Watch overturns "technical impossibility" |url=https://www.4gamer.net/games/999/G999905/20220303097/ |access-date=21 March 2025 |website=[[4Gamer.net]] |language=ja}}</ref><ref name="Yokoi 1996" /> This led to the idea of using a [[segmented liquid-crystal display]] (LCD), like those in calculators, to create a smaller device with sharper, clearer graphics, though limited to a single game.<ref name="Retro Gamer 163">{{Cite magazine |year=2016 |title=Satoru Okada talks Game & Watch, Game Boy and Nintendo DS development |url=http://www.japanesenintendo.com/post/155107688449 |url-status=usurped |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170101232208/http://www.japanesenintendo.com/post/155107688449 |archive-date=1 January 2017 |access-date=1 January 2017 |magazine=[[Retro Gamer]] |issue=163}}</ref><ref name="Parish 2011" />

This experience would inspire Yokoi's design philosophy of {{Nihongo foot|"[[lateral thinking]] with withered technology",|枯れた技術の水平思考|Kareta Gijutsu no Suihei Shikō|lead=yes|group=lower-alpha}} which emphasized creative uses of mature, cost-effective technology. This principle would continue to be embraced by Nintendo in the following decades.<ref name="McFarren 20162" /> At the time, fierce competition in the calculator market between [[Sharp Corporation|Sharp]] and [[Casio]] had created a surplus of LCDs and semiconductors, creating an opportunity to repurpose these components for gaming.<ref name="Parish 2011">{{Cite web |last=Parish |first=Jeremy |date=31 March 2011 |title=The Troubled Past and Challenging Future of Nintendo 3DS: What the 3DS owes to Virtual Boy (and how it's different) |url=http://www.1up.com/features/nintendo-3ds-past-present-future |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120711185404/http://www.1up.com/features/nintendo-3ds-past-present-future |archive-date=11 July 2012 |website=[[1Up Network|1up.com]]}}</ref><ref name="McFarren 20162">{{Cite book |last=McFarren |first=Damien |title=Videogames Hardware Handbook |publisher=[[Imagine Publishing]] |year=2016 |isbn=978-1-78546-239-9 |edition=2nd |volume=1 |location=Bournemouth |pages=157–163}}</ref>

Yokoi pitched the idea to Nintendo president [[Hiroshi Yamauchi]] while driving him to a business meeting after the company chauffeur called in sick. Yamauchi's meeting happened to be with Sharp's CEO, and the two leaders discussed the concept. Within a week, Yokoi was invited to a meeting between Nintendo and Sharp, where approval was given to develop a calculator-sized gaming device.<ref name="vice yokoi" />

Yamauchi, however, assigned the project to [[Nintendo Research & Development 2]] (R&D2), which was established in 1978 to focus on electronic projects like the [[Color TV-Game]], while R&D1 was to continue to focus on creating physical toys and games. R&D2 rejected the idea, claiming it was too difficult to implement. Undeterred, Yokoi and Okada continued developing the concept on their own. Yokoi created a mock-up of the first game, ''[[Ball (video game)|Ball]]'', and demonstrated it to Okada by shining light through cut-out paper shapes. Okada then built a working prototype, buying a [[TK-80]] computer, teaching himself to program for the [[Intel 8080]], and designing the necessary electronics.<ref name="Kurokawa 2022" />

When they presented the prototype to employees at Sharp, they also dismissed the project, saying it wasn't feasible to scale it down to calculator size. Yokoi and Okada left the prototype with Sharp, asking them to play with it for a while, which convinced Sharp employees of the game's potential and led them to develop a compatible display.<ref name="Kurokawa 2022" /> The final device was powered by a [[4-bit computing|4-bit]] [[microcontroller]] from Sharp's SM5xx family containing an LCD screen driver and an integrated [[Read-only memory|ROM]] and RAM. The SM5A and SM510–SM512 are rated for a cycle time 61 [[microseconds]] (μs) and externally connect to a standard 32.768&nbsp;[[kHz]] [[crystal oscillator]] (commonly used in [[quartz clock]]s) and an internal clock generator with a 15-stage [[Frequency divider#Digital|frequency divider]].<ref name=":1" /> Note: a 61&nbsp;μs cycle time corresponds to a [[clock rate]] of roughly 16.384&nbsp;kHz (half of 32.768&nbsp;kHz).

The Game & Watch name reflected its dual functionality: it combined a single game with a digital clock.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Game & Watch (Franchise) |url=https://www.giantbomb.com/game-watch/3025-1183/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210127221433/https://www.giantbomb.com/game-watch/3025-1183/ |archive-date=27 January 2021 |access-date=15 June 2020 |website=[[Giant Bomb]] |language=en}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |date=16 May 2013 |title=Digital game + digital clock = Game & Watch! |url=https://recollectionsofplay.wordpress.com/2013/05/15/why-game-and-watch-when-you-could-game-watch/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200615190339/https://recollectionsofplay.wordpress.com/2013/05/15/why-game-and-watch-when-you-could-game-watch/ |archive-date=15 June 2020 |access-date=15 June 2020 |website=Recollections of Play |language=en}}</ref> The first game, ''Ball'', had modest initial sales but enough success for Yamauchi to greenlight three more games: ''Flagman'', ''Vermin'', and ''Fire''.<ref name="Kurokawa 2022" /> This eventually led to dozens of titles over the next decade.<ref name="G&W History">{{Cite web |date=24 February 2010 |title=Feature: The History of the Nintendo Game & Watch |url=http://www.nintendolife.com/news/2010/02/feature_the_history_of_the_nintendo_game_and_watch |access-date=26 January 2019 |website=[[Gamer Network#Nintendo Life|Nintendo Life]] |language=en-GB}}</ref>

In North America, the games were initially launched through [[Mego Corporation]] as the '''Time-Out''' series. The series included ''Ball'' (renamed ''Toss-Up''), ''Flagman'', ''Vermin'' (renamed ''Exterminator''), and ''Fire'' (retitled ''Fireman Fireman''). This partnership ended within a year, and Nintendo of America began distributing the series under their original titles.<ref name="Salvador 2024">{{Cite web |last=Salvador |first=Phil |title=Is This the First Nintendo Commercial? |url=https://gamehistory.org/mego-time-out-commercial/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20240627061609/https://gamehistory.org/mego-time-out-commercial/ |archive-date=27 June 2024 |access-date=2 July 2024 |website=[[Video Game History Foundation]] |date=13 June 2024 |ref=Salvador 2024}}</ref>

[[File:Nintendo Donkey Kong Game and Watch.png|thumb|''[[List of LCD games featuring Mario|Donkey Kong]]'', showing its revolutionary [[D-pad]] and multi-screen clamshell design]]

Starting in 1981, Game & Watch devices incorporated an alarm function, enhancing their practicality as a watch.<ref>{{Cite web |date=24 February 2010 |title=Feature: The History of the Nintendo Game & Watch |url=https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2010/02/feature_the_history_of_the_nintendo_game_and_watch |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20200615190339/https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2010/02/feature_the_history_of_the_nintendo_game_and_watch |archive-date=15 June 2020 |access-date=15 June 2020 |website=Nintendo Life |language=en-GB}}</ref> By mid-1982, limitations of single-screen LCDs led to the creation of the Multi Screen Series, which effectively doubled the gameplay area. ''[[List of Game & Watch games#Oil Panic|Oil Panic]]'' launched the series. Shortly thereafter, Nintendo's highly successful [[Donkey Kong (1981 video game)|''Donkey Kong'' arcade game]] was adapted for the Multi Screen format, requiring an alternative to the traditional [[joystick]], which was impractical for handheld gaming. This challenge led to the invention of the now-iconic [[D-pad]], a flat, four-way directional control that allowed for precise movement with minimal space requirements.<ref name="G&W History" /><ref name="hardware_handbook_1">{{Cite magazine |last=McFerran |first=Damien |date=2016 |title=Game Boy |magazine=[[Retro Gamer|Videogames Hardware Handbook Vol 1. (2nd RE)]] |pages=157–163}}</ref><ref name="boar">{{Cite web |last=Goodall |first=Reece |date=13 June 2020 |title=Concept to Console: Game & Watch |url=https://theboar.org/2020/06/concept-to-console-game-and-watch-nintendo-smash-bros/ |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210301095838/https://theboar.org/2020/06/concept-to-console-game-and-watch-nintendo-smash-bros/ |archive-date=1 March 2021 |access-date=26 October 2020 |website=TheBoar.org}}</ref> The D-pad design was later honored with a [[Technology and Engineering Emmy Awards|Technology & Engineering Emmy Award]] in 2008.<ref>{{Cite web |date=9 January 2008 |title=Nintendo Wins Emmy For DS And Wii Engineering &#124; Technology &#124; Sky News |url=http://news.sky.com/skynews/Home/Sky-News-Archive/Article/20080641299976 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://archive.today/20120711165613/http://news.sky.com/skynews/Home/Sky-News-Archive/Article/20080641299976 |archive-date=11 July 2012 |access-date=30 August 2010 |publisher=[[Sky News]]}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |last=Magrino |first=Tom |date=8 January 2008 |title=CES '08: Nintendo wins second Emmy - News at GameSpot |url=http://www.gamespot.com/articles/ces-08-nintendo-wins-second-emmy/1100-6184421/ |access-date=28 April 2016 |publisher=[[GameSpot]]}}</ref> The [[clamshell design]] of the Multi Screen Series would later inspire the [[Game Boy Advance SP]], [[Nintendo DS]], and [[Nintendo 3DS]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=8 September 2020 |title=Feature: How Nintendo's Game & Watch Took "Withered Technology" And Turned It Into A Million-Seller |url=https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2020/09/feature_how_nintendos_game_and_watch_took_withered_technology_and_turned_it_into_a_million-seller |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201017092404/https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2020/09/feature_how_nintendos_game_and_watch_took_withered_technology_and_turned_it_into_a_million-seller |archive-date=17 October 2020 |access-date=24 October 2020 |website=Nintendo Life |language=en-GB}}</ref>

The Game & Watch series was a monumental success. Yokoi had initially aimed to sell 100,000 units, but Nintendo ultimately sold 43.4 million units worldwide—12.87 million in Japan and 30.53 million overseas.<ref name="iwataasks2">{{Cite web |title=Iwata Asks: Game & Watch: 4. Absorbed in Development. |url=http://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/#/clubn/game-and-watch-ball-reward/0/3 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110518183353/http://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/#/clubn/game-and-watch-ball-reward/0/3 |archive-date=18 May 2011 |access-date=23 June 2013 |publisher=[[Nintendo|Nintendo of America]]}}</ref> The device also significantly improved Nintendo's financial standing. Before its release in 1980, the company reportedly had debts of 7 to 8 billion yen. By 1981, however, largely due to the Game & Watch's success, Nintendo had paid off its debts and put about 4 billion yen in the bank.<ref name="Yokoi 1996">{{Cite magazine |last=Yokoi |first=Gunpei |date=November 1996 |title=ゲームボーイを開発した伝説の技術者・横井軍平「私はなぜ任天堂を辞めたか」|trans-title=Gunpei Yokoi, the legendary engineer who developed the Game Boy: "Why I left Nintendo" |url=https://bunshun.jp/articles/-/6659 |access-date=20 March 2025 |magazine=[[Bungeishunjū]] |language=ja}}</ref> It marked the company's first major worldwide success with a [[Nintendo video game consoles|Nintendo video game product]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=Wii.com – Iwata Asks: Super Mario Bros. 25th Anniversary |url=http://us.wii.com/iwata_asks/mario25th/vol2_page1.jsp |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20111231005824/http://us.wii.com/iwata_asks/mario25th/vol2_page1.jsp |archive-date=31 December 2011 |access-date=23 March 2011 |publisher=Us.wii.com}}</ref>

== Series ==
{{Main|List of Game & Watch games}}
[[File:Chef - Game&Watch - Nintendo.jpg|thumb|''Chef'', a Game & Watch Wide Screen series device]]
Over the initial eleven-year life span of the ''Game & Watch'' line, 60 different games were produced for sale.<ref name="G&W.ch">{{Cite web |date=2 January 2009 |title=Super Mario Bros. YM-901 |url=http://www.gameandwatch.ch/en/faqs/super-mario-bros-ym-901.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110309015902/http://www.gameandwatch.ch/en/faqs/super-mario-bros-ym-901.html |archive-date=9 March 2011 |access-date=1 July 2009}}</ref> These games came in eleven different series, each with a different form-factor:<ref name="McFarren 20162" />

* Silver (1980) – the first version of the Game & Watch
* Gold (1981) – added alarm clock function, static color backgrounds, and a built-in wire loop stand
* Wide Screen (1981–1982) – introduced a 30% larger LCD panel in a slightly wider form factor
* Vertical Multi Screen (1982–1989) – two LCD panels in a vertical folding clamshell unit
* Horizontal Multi Screen (1983) – two LCD panels in a right-to-left opening clamshell unit
* New Wide Screen (1982–1991) – an updated version of the Wide Screen series; used colorful metal faceplates for each game
* Tabletop (1983) – made to compete with [[Coleco]]; these actually do not use VFD (Vacuum Fluorescent Display) screens but regular LCD screens like all other Game & Watch games. Ambient light transmitted through the translucent top illuminates an integrated mirror which bounces the light onto the back of the display. The polarizer film is flipped front to back, creating a full blackened LCD screen in power-off mode, when LCD segments are turned on these will become transparent holes, allowing light passing through them from the mirror on the back. The colors come from a colored overlay which make the holes appear colored.
* Panorama (1983–1984) - similar design to the Tabletop units, but folded into a compact size via an articulated hinge
* Super Color (1984) – used a long portrait-oriented LCD panel with color overlays to color the display elements
* Micro Vs. System (1984) – used a wide horizontal LCD panel; allowed 2-player games via two external wired control pads
* Crystal Screen (1986) – used a unique large see-through LCD panel with no reflective background; all three games were later re-released as New Wide Screen titles

Titles included characters from other Nintendo franchises, including ''[[The Legend of Zelda LCD games#Game & Watch: Zelda|The Legend of Zelda]]'' and ''[[List of Game & Watch games#Mario Bros.|Mario Bros.]]'' Nintendo also licensed third-party characters including [[Mickey Mouse]].

Among the 60 games was one "prize" game, a version of ''[[Super Mario Bros.]]'' that came in a yellow plastic case modeled after the Disk-kun character Nintendo used to advertise their [[Famicom Disk System]].<ref name=":0">{{cite web | title=ファミコン必勝本 No. 037( 1987年 12月 18日号) | date=18 December 1987 | url=https://archive.org/details/no.-037-1987-12-18/%E3%83%95%E3%82%A1%E3%83%9F%E3%82%B3%E3%83%B3%E5%BF%85%E5%8B%9D%E6%9C%ACNo.037%281987%E5%B9%B412%E6%9C%8818%E6%97%A5%E5%8F%B7%29/page/n10/mode/1up}}</ref><ref name="HHmuseum2">{{Cite web |title=Nintendo Super Mario Bros Promo |url=http://www.handheldmuseum.com/Nintendo/SMBPromo.htm |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210115233757/https://www.handheldmuseum.com/Nintendo/SMBPromo.htm |archive-date=15 January 2021 |access-date=1 July 2009}}</ref> These games were never sold, but instead given away to 10,000 winners of Nintendo's ''[[F-1 Grand Prix (video game series)|F-1 Grand Prix]]'' tournament.<ref name=":0"/><ref name="G&W.ch" />

The Game & Watch series sold 14 million units worldwide during its first year of release by 1981.<ref>{{Cite web |title=昔(1970年代）のテレビゲームは何台売れた？|trans-title=How many old (1970s) video games sold? |url=https://www.ne.jp/asahi/cvs/odyssey/hyperlink/setframe_dataroom_numbers1.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20140109231757/http://www.ne.jp/asahi/cvs/odyssey/hyperlink/setframe_dataroom_numbers1.html |archive-date=9 January 2014 |access-date=16 April 2021 |website=Classic Videogame Station Odyssey |language=ja}}</ref> The Game & Watch version of ''[[Donkey Kong (1981 video game)|Donkey Kong]]'' released in 1982 sold 8 million units.<ref name="Epstein">{{Cite book |last=Epstein |first=David |author-link=David Epstein (journalist) |title=[[Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World]] |date=27 June 2019 |publisher=[[Pan Books|Pan Macmillan]] |isbn=978-1-5098-4351-0 |page=196 |chapter=Chapter 9: Lateral Thinking with Withered Technology |quote=The Donkey Kong Game & Watch was released in 1982 and alone sold eight million units. |chapter-url=https://books.google.com/books?id=oEGCDwAAQBAJ&pg=PT196}}</ref> ''[[List of Game & Watch games#Mario the Juggler|Mario the Juggler]]'' was the last LCD installment in the ''Game & Watch'' series released in 1991 and is a tribute to the first game, ''Ball''.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Obscure Pixels – Nintendo Game&Watch |url=http://homepages.ihug.co.nz/~pinwhiz/g&w.htm |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20181130000851/http://homepages.ihug.co.nz/~pinwhiz/g%26w.htm |archive-date=30 November 2018 |access-date=11 July 2009}}</ref><ref name="McFarren 20162" />

== Game A and Game B ==
Most of the titles have a "GAME A" and a "GAME B" button. Game B is generally a faster, more difficult version of Game A, although exceptions do exist:

* In ''Squish'', Game B is radically different from Game A—the player must touch aliens to eliminate them as opposed to avoiding moving walls.<ref>{{Cite web |title=gw / squish |url=http://www.gameandwatch.com/screen/multiscreen/squish/index.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090625012323/http://www.gameandwatch.com/screen/multiscreen/squish/index.html |archive-date=25 June 2009 |access-date=11 July 2009}}</ref>
* In ''Flagman'', Game B is a mode where the player has to press the right button within a certain amount of time, not memorize patterns.
* In ''Judge'', ''Boxing'', ''Donkey Kong 3'', and ''Donkey Kong Hockey'', Game B is a two-player version of Game A.<ref>{{Cite web |last=van Spanje |first=Martin |title=#026: Judge – a Nintendo Game & Watch collection |url=http://p-edge.nl/parachuter/game_watch_games/026_judge |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090715005513/http://p-edge.nl/parachuter/game_watch_games/026_judge |archive-date=15 July 2009 |access-date=11 July 2009}}</ref>
* In ''Climber'',<ref>{{Cite web |title=gw / climber |url=http://www.gameandwatch.com/screen/crystal/climber/index.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20091007051623/http://www.gameandwatch.com/screen/crystal/climber/index.html |archive-date=7 October 2009 |access-date=11 July 2009}}</ref> ''Balloon Fight'',<ref>{{Cite web |title=gw / balloon fight |url=http://www.gameandwatch.com/screen/crystal/balloonfight/index.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20091007171858/http://www.gameandwatch.com/screen/crystal/balloonfight/index.html |archive-date=7 October 2009 |access-date=11 July 2009}}</ref> and ''Super Mario Bros.'',<ref>{{Cite web |title=gw / super mario bros. |url=http://www.gameandwatch.com/screen/crystal/supermario/index.html |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090908063728/http://www.gameandwatch.com/screen/crystal/supermario/index.html |archive-date=8 September 2009 |access-date=11 July 2009}}</ref><ref name="HHmuseum2"/> there is no Game B button.

In most cases, both Game A and Game B would increase in speed and/or difficulty as the player progressed, with Game B starting at the level that Game A would reach at 200 points.

== Legacy, ports and remakes ==
[[File:Электроника ИМ 02.jpg|thumb|[[Elektronika]] IM-02 ([[Well, Just You Wait!]]), a clone of the Game & Watch from the [[Soviet Union]]]]

The Game & Watch series helped to popularize handheld video games, inspiring imitators like [[Tiger Electronics]], [[MGA Entertainment|Micro Games of America]], [[Konami]] and [[Elektronika]], which released the IM-02 series in the [[Soviet Union]].<ref>{{Cite web |last=Richter |first=Darmon |date=3 May 2023 |title=How A Nintendo Game & Watch Bootleg Enraptured A Generation Of Russian Kids |url=https://www.timeextension.com/features/how-a-nintendo-game-and-watch-bootleg-enraptured-a-generation-of-russian-kids |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230503140647/https://www.timeextension.com/features/how-a-nintendo-game-and-watch-bootleg-enraptured-a-generation-of-russian-kids |archive-date=3 May 2023 |access-date=3 May 2023 |website=[[Gamer Network#Time Extension|Time Extension]] |publisher=Hookshot Media}}</ref>

[[File:bassmate_31.jpg|thumb|[[Fishing]] [[Computer]] Models of the Bassmate, Bassmate II and WalleyeMate]]

[[Nintendo]] also used the core technology from the Game & Watch line for the Bassmate Computer, a [[Mobile device|handheld]] [[Database|databank]] intended for [[bass fishing]]. The Bassmate was released in 1984 by Telko under [[White-label product|various brand names]], with Nintendo acting as the [[original design manufacturer]].<ref>{{Cite web |title=Bassmate Computer – a new member on the Game & Watch family tree |url=http://blog.beforemario.com/2013/06/bassmate-computer-new-member-on-game.html |access-date=2025-12-07 |website=[[Before Mario]]}}</ref>
 
After this initial model, Telko and its partners expanded the concept into several related [[fishing]] computers—including the Bassmate II, WalleyeMate, and Troutmate—all of which were produced without Nintendo's involvement. These later units reused the same basic [[Electronic hardware|hardware]] and [[Computing platform|software platform]] created for the original Bassmate but were engineered and manufactured entirely by third‑party companies. Each model targeted a different fishing niche—[[Bass (fish)|bass]], [[walleye]], or [[trout]]—and, like the first Bassmate, was sold under multiple brand names such as Telko, KMV, and Probe 2000.<ref>{{Cite web |title=How the Bassmate Computer came to be |url=http://blog.beforemario.com/2013/06/how-bassmate-computer-came-to-be.html |access-date=2025-12-07 |website=Before Mario}}</ref>

[[File:Nintendo Mini Classics Super Mario Bros y Parachute 2.jpg|thumb|[[Nintendo Mini Classics]]]]

Nintendo revived the series between 1995 and 2002 with the [[List of Game & Watch games#Compilations|''Game & Watch Gallery'' series]] for [[Game Boy]], [[Game Boy Color]] and [[Game Boy Advance]] featuring original ports and modernized [[Mario (franchise)|Mario]]-themed versions. Starting in 1998, smaller [[LCD]] versions of ten Game & Watch titles were re-released as the [[Nintendo Mini Classics]]. In 2001, Nintendo bundled ''Manhole-e'' with the [[Nintendo e-Reader]], though a planned line of e-Reader Game & Watch cards never materialized.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Game & Watch Collection: Manhole Release Information for e-Reader |url=http://www.gamefaqs.com/ereader/915448-game-and-watch-collection-manhole/data |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131227124459/http://www.gamefaqs.com/ereader/915448-game-and-watch-collection-manhole/data |archive-date=27 December 2013 |access-date=6 July 2013 |publisher=[[GameFAQs]]}}</ref>

From 2006 to 2010, Nintendo produced the ''Game & Watch Collection'' series for the [[Nintendo DS]], initially exclusive to [[Club Nintendo]]. Between 2009 and 2010, nine Game & Watch titles were released on [[List of DSiWare games|DSiWare]].<ref>{{Cite web |date=22 March 2010 |title=Game & Watch: Mario's Cement Factory Release Information for DS |url=http://www.gamefaqs.com/ds/961347-game-and-watch-marios-cement-factory/data |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160918100125/http://www.gamefaqs.com/ds/961347-game-and-watch-marios-cement-factory/data |archive-date=18 September 2016 |access-date=6 July 2013 |publisher=GameFAQs}}</ref><ref>{{Cite web |date=10 July 2009 |title=GAME&WATCH |url=https://www.nintendo.co.jp/ds/dsiware/game_and_watch/index.html |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171124055820/https://www.nintendo.co.jp/ds/dsiware/game_and_watch/index.html |archive-date=24 November 2017 |access-date=11 July 2009 |publisher=[[Nintendo]].co.jp}}</ref> In the [[Nintendo Game Card|DS game]], ''[[Cooking Guide: Can't Decide What to Eat?]]'', the player can unlock the game ''[[List of Game & Watch games#Chef|Chef]]''.<ref>{{Cite web |title=Personal Trainer: Cooking – Cheats |url=https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/ds/946314-personal-trainer-cooking/cheats+%26cd%3D1%26hl%3Den%26ct%3Dclnk%26gl%3Dus |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20201028212101/https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/ds/946314-personal-trainer-cooking/cheats+%26cd%3D1%26hl%3Den%26ct%3Dclnk%26gl%3Dus |archive-date=28 October 2020 |access-date=25 October 2020}}</ref>
